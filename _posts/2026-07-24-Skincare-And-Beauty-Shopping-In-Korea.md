<p>Skincare is one of the few things almost every visitor to Korea buys, and one of the easiest to buy badly. The shops are dense, generous with samples and built around a routine most newcomers only half understand, so it is common to fly home with products you never open. This is how the shops work, and how to buy things you will actually use.</p>


<h3>Why Korea Became a Skincare Centre</h3>

<p>Two things explain it. The first is an unusually competitive domestic market with a short product cycle: brands here iterate quickly, and a formula that does not sell is replaced rather than defended. Formats that are now normal worldwide, including light watery essences and cushion foundations, reached the mainstream in Korea first.</p>

<p>The second is cultural. Skincare is treated as ordinary maintenance rather than indulgence, and it is not gendered to the degree it is elsewhere. Men's ranges are standard in any shop, not a niche shelf. That large everyday customer base sustains the range you see.</p>

<p>Set aside the marketing story, though. There is no single ingredient behind so-called Korean skin. There is consistency, a strong habit of sun avoidance, and a culture of using gentle products often rather than harsh ones occasionally. That is the part you can take home.</p>


<h3>Single-Brand Shops and Multi-Brand Retailers Are Different Trips</h3>

<p>Single-brand shops &mdash; Innisfree, Nature Republic, Etude, Missha and others &mdash; sell only their own line. Staff know that range in depth, promotions are frequent, and it is the easier place to ask a specific question. The limit is obvious: you can only compare within one house.</p>

<p>Multi-brand retailers, of which Olive Young is the one everybody eventually walks into, stock dozens of brands side by side. This is where real comparison happens, and where you see what is selling rather than what one brand wants to push. Department counters sit above both for premium ranges, and duty-free is its own track.</p>

<p>The efficient approach is to browse a multi-brand shop first to work out what suits you, then buy wherever it is cheapest, including back at the single-brand shop if it is running a promotion. Districts such as Myeongdong and Hongdae cluster both types within a few streets of each other, and both are straightforward to reach using our <a href="/journal/Getting-Around-Korea-Public-Transport-Guide/">public transport guide</a>.</p>


<h3>Testers and Samples Are Part of the System</h3>

<p>Testers are standard, not a favour. Most products have an opened version beside them and you are expected to try it. Some shops set out two baskets at the entrance: one meaning you want help, one meaning you are browsing alone. Taking the browsing basket is normal, not rude.</p>

<p>The etiquette is simple. Use the spatula or applicator provided rather than your fingers, test on the back of your hand or your inner forearm rather than your face, and pat rather than rub. If a member of staff offers help and you would rather look on your own, a polite 괜찮아요 (gwaenchanayo), meaning "it's fine", is understood and expected.</p>

<p>Samples are usually added at the till as small foil sachets. They are more useful than they look, because the sensible way to try an unfamiliar product is on a small patch of skin over several days before committing to a full size. Ask for samples of anything you are undecided about.</p>


<h3>The Layered Routine, Explained Honestly</h3>

<p>The much-quoted ten-step routine is a sequence, not a shopping list. The logic is thin to thick, water-based before oil-based, so each layer can reach the skin instead of sitting on something heavier. In long form it runs: oil cleanser, water-based cleanser, exfoliant, toner, essence, serum, sheet mask, eye cream, moisturiser, sunscreen.</p>

<p>Very few people do all of that daily, including in Korea. A short version handles most needs perfectly well:</p>

<ul>
<li>A gentle cleanser, morning and evening</li>
<li>One hydrating layer &mdash; a toner or an essence, not necessarily both</li>
<li>A moisturiser suited to the season</li>
<li>Sunscreen every morning</li>
</ul>

<p>The real risk of the long routine is not cost but overlap. Stacking several active ingredients at once is a common cause of irritation, and when your skin reacts you cannot tell which product caused it. Introduce one product at a time and give it a fortnight. That habit does more than any specific purchase.</p>


<h3>Sheet Masks Are the Sensible First Purchase</h3>

<p>If you buy one category, make it sheet masks. They are inexpensive, flat enough to pack, difficult to misuse and easy to give away, which makes them the lowest-risk thing in the shop. They are also the standard small gift to bring home.</p>

<p>It is worth being clear about what they do. A sheet mask holds a hydrating essence against the skin for fifteen or twenty minutes, and afterwards skin looks plumper and calmer for a while. That is a real effect and a temporary one, not a treatment.</p>

<p>Buy a mixed pack rather than a box of one type. You will find out which textures and scents you enjoy, and enjoyment determines whether the rest gets used or sits in a drawer.</p>


<h3>Sunscreen Is the Part That Matters Year-Round</h3>

<p>If you take one habit home, take this one. Korean sunscreens are known for cosmetic elegance &mdash; light textures, minimal white cast, comfortable under makeup &mdash; and that matters more than it sounds. Sunscreen works only if you apply enough and reapply, and people only do that with a product they do not mind wearing.</p>

<p>Two figures appear on the packaging. SPF describes protection against UVB, the wavelength mainly responsible for burning. The PA rating, written as PA+ through to PA++++, describes protection against UVA, which is associated with longer-term skin ageing. More plus signs means more UVA protection.</p>

<p>The common misconception is that sunscreen is a summer product. UVA passes through cloud and glass, so the exposure continues on grey days and in winter. Given how sharply the light and humidity shift across the year here, as set out in our <a href="/journal/Four-Seasons-In-Korea-What-To-Expect/">guide to the four seasons</a>, it is reasonable to change the texture by season while keeping the habit constant.</p>


<h3>Cushion Compacts and Reading a Korean Label</h3>

<p>The cushion compact is a Korean invention: liquid base soaked into a sponge inside a compact, applied with a puff. Its advantage is touch-ups, which suits a humid summer. Coverage is light to medium, the finish dewy. Cushions list an SPF figure, but the layer you apply is far too thin to count as sun protection, so treat it as a bonus, not a substitute.</p>

<p>Shade ranges have historically been narrow and skewed light, though that is improving. Swatch on your jawline and step towards daylight, because shop lighting flatters everything. Shade codes usually combine a number with an undertone letter, and staff will match you if you ask.</p>

<p>The front of a box is marketing; the back is information. This is where reading Hangul earns its keep, and our <a href="/journal/Hangul-And-First-Korean-Phrases/">alphabet guide</a> covers enough to sound out a label. A few terms recur constantly: 수분 (subun) means moisture or hydration, 미백 (mibaek) refers to brightening, 주름 (jureum) means wrinkles, 진정 (jinjeong) means soothing, and 무향 (muhyang) means fragrance-free. Products are also labelled with a manufacture date, 제조 (jejo), and a use-by date, 사용기한 (sayonggihan), which is worth checking on anything from a discount bin.</p>


<h3>Tax Refunds, and Not Buying Too Much</h3>

<p>Short-term visitors can generally reclaim VAT on qualifying purchases, by one of two routes. Larger shops often operate immediate refund, where the deduction is applied at the till when you show your passport. Otherwise you keep the tax-free receipt and process it at a kiosk before departure, sometimes with the goods available for inspection. Carry your passport, keep receipts together, and allow time at the airport. Thresholds and procedures change, so confirm current terms in the shop.</p>

<p>The more useful discipline comes before any of that. Skincare is mostly water and it does expire, so a large haul of unfamiliar products is often a slow waste. Buy small sizes of anything untried, full sizes only of what you know works, and stock up on the boring things &mdash; sunscreen, cleanser, sheet masks &mdash; that get finished.</p>

<p>One practical note on getting it all home. Anything you buy can be sent on to your accommodation rather than carried around for the rest of the day, which is worth knowing if you are shopping mid-trip. The mechanics are covered in our <a href="/journal/Recycling-Deliveries-And-Daily-Logistics/">guide to deliveries and daily logistics</a>.</p>
