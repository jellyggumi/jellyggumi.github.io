<h3>Banchan Is Structural, Not a Starter</h3>

<p>The small dishes that arrive before your main order are banchan, and the most common misreading is treating them as an appetiser course to finish before the real food comes. They are not a first course. They stay on the table and are eaten alongside everything else, providing contrast in salt, acidity and texture against the main dish.</p>

<p>Because they are part of the meal rather than a prelude, pacing them matters. Clearing every dish before your main arrives leaves you eating the main course without the balance it was designed around. Refills are commonly available on request, though what is offered varies by restaurant and by region.</p>


<h3>Seniority Shapes the Table</h3>

<p>Age and seniority quietly organise a Korean meal. The most senior person at the table typically begins eating first, and others follow shortly after. It is a small gesture and easy to observe simply by waiting a beat before picking up your spoon.</p>

<p>Pouring follows a similar logic. Pouring for others rather than yourself is normal, and when receiving a drink from someone senior it is polite to hold your glass with both hands or to support your pouring arm with the other hand. These conventions vary by generation and by how formal the setting is, and among close friends they relax considerably.</p>


<h3>Shared Dishes and Individual Bowls</h3>

<p>Many Korean dishes are served communally in the centre of the table, while rice and soup usually come in individual bowls. Stews such as jjigae are often eaten directly from the shared pot in casual settings, though many restaurants now provide serving spoons, and asking for one is unremarkable.</p>

<p>The distinction between soup words is worth knowing. Guk tends to be a lighter, more broth-forward soup served as part of a meal, tang generally refers to a heartier, longer-simmered soup, and jjigae is thicker, saltier and built for sharing. The boundaries are not rigid and usage overlaps, but the rough hierarchy helps when reading a menu.</p>


<h3>Spoons, Chopsticks and What Not to Do</h3>

<p>Rice and soup are usually eaten with a spoon, while chopsticks handle side dishes and pieces of meat. Holding both at once in the same hand is generally avoided. Korean chopsticks are commonly metal and slightly heavier than wooden ones, which takes a short adjustment.</p>

<p>Sticking chopsticks upright into a bowl of rice is the one thing to genuinely avoid, as the shape carries funerary associations. Lifting a rice bowl off the table to eat from it is also less common than in some neighbouring food cultures. These are conventions rather than laws, and no one expects a visitor to be flawless.</p>


<h3>How Ordering and Paying Work</h3>

<p>Many restaurants have a call button at the table, and pressing it to summon a server is the expected behaviour rather than an imposition. Where there is no button, calling out to staff is normal and not considered rude in a casual setting.</p>

<p>Payment is frequently made at a counter near the entrance rather than at the table, and one person often pays for the group and settles up afterwards. Tipping is not customary, and adding one can create confusion rather than goodwill. Water and basic side dishes are typically included rather than charged separately.</p>


<h3>Self-Serve Stations and Shoes-Off Seating</h3>

<p>Water, cups, cutlery, and sometimes extra side dishes are often self-serve from a station along the wall or a drawer built into the table. Looking for the drawer under the table edge before asking for utensils saves a wait, since it is where cutlery frequently lives.</p>

<p>Some restaurants use raised floor seating where shoes come off before stepping up. Where this is the case it is usually obvious from the shoe racks and the step, and following what other diners are doing is the simplest guide.</p>


<h3>The Shape of a Korean Meal</h3>

<p>A Korean meal generally arrives close to all at once rather than in sequenced courses. The main dish, rice, soup and banchan share the table simultaneously, and you move between them by choice rather than by order. Coming from a coursed dining tradition, this can feel like everything has landed early, when in fact it has landed correctly.</p>

<p>Two further things surprise visitors. Delivery is deeply normal across an enormous range of food, and eating alone is entirely unremarkable, with many restaurants and menus built around single diners. Both reflect a food culture that is convenient and unfussy as much as it is communal.</p>
