<p>A Korean table is organised by conventions nobody states out loud. Where you sit, who lifts a spoon first, which hand holds the bottle and who pays are all settled before the food arrives. Little of it is enforced on visitors, and none of it is complicated.</p>


<h3>Banchan Is Structural, Not a Starter</h3>

<p>The small dishes that arrive before your main order are 반찬 (banchan), and the most common misreading is treating them as an appetiser course to finish before the real food comes. They are not a first course. They stay on the table and are eaten alongside everything else, providing contrast in salt, acidity and texture against the main dish.</p>

<p>Because they are part of the meal rather than a prelude, pacing them matters. Clearing every dish before your main arrives leaves you eating the main course without the balance it was designed around. Refills are commonly available on request, though what is offered varies by restaurant and by region.</p>

<p>They are not free extras either. Korean rice is deliberately plain, and the seasoning of the meal lives in the dishes around it, so removing them leaves the plate incomplete rather than less generous. To refill, point at the empty dish and say 더 주세요 (deo juseyo).</p>


<h3>Seniority Decides Who Sits and Who Begins</h3>

<p>Age and seniority quietly organise a Korean meal before anyone eats. The best seat &mdash; 상석 (sangseok), furthest from the door &mdash; belongs to the most senior person, and the most junior sits nearest the door. If someone gestures you towards a chair, take it.</p>

<p>The most senior person at the table typically begins eating first, and others follow shortly after. It is a small gesture and easy to observe simply by waiting a beat before picking up your spoon. Many people say 잘 먹겠습니다 (jal meokgetseumnida) before starting, and the junior end of the table lays out the cutlery and pours the water. How rank gets worked out is covered in our post on <a href="/journal/Korean-Age-Names-And-How-Addresses-Work/">Korean age, names and how addresses work</a>.</p>


<h3>Pouring, Receiving and Turning Away</h3>

<p>Pouring follows a similar logic. Pouring for others rather than yourself is normal, and when receiving a drink from someone senior it is polite to hold your glass with both hands or to support your pouring arm with the other hand. These conventions vary by generation and by how formal the setting is, and among close friends they relax considerably.</p>

<p>The rule underneath is that you do not pour your own drink. The bottle circulates, and an empty glass is a quiet request &mdash; the practice is to wait until one is genuinely empty rather than topping it up. If yours is empty, fill someone else's and it comes back.</p>

<p>A junior person drinking in front of someone senior turns head and shoulders slightly away, shielding the glass, on the logic that swallowing is private. Refusing a drink is fine, and phrasing decides it: 저는 술을 못 마셔요 (jeoneun sureul mot masyeoyo), "I can't drink alcohol", is heard as a fact and closes the subject. Accepting the pour, toasting with 건배 (geonbae) and leaving the glass full works too, since a full glass is never refilled.</p>


<h3>Spoons, Chopsticks and What Not to Do</h3>

<p>Rice and soup are usually eaten with a spoon, while chopsticks handle side dishes and pieces of meat. Holding both at once in the same hand is generally avoided. Korean chopsticks are commonly metal and slightly heavier than wooden ones, which takes a short adjustment.</p>

<p>Sticking chopsticks upright into a bowl of rice is the one thing to genuinely avoid, as the shape carries funerary associations: it echoes 제사 (jesa), the ancestral rite, where a spoon stands upright in rice set out for the dead. Passing food chopstick-to-chopstick is the other real taboo. Put the piece on the person's plate instead.</p>

<p>Lifting a rice bowl off the table to eat from it is also less common than in some neighbouring food cultures. The bowl stays down and the spoon travels up, which is why the spoon does so much of the work here. These are conventions rather than laws, and no one expects a visitor to be flawless.</p>

<h3>Shared Dishes and the Communal Spoon</h3>

<p>Many Korean dishes are served communally in the centre of the table, while rice and soup usually come in individual bowls. Stews such as jjigae are often eaten directly from the shared pot in casual settings, though many restaurants now provide serving spoons, and asking for one is unremarkable. 앞접시 주세요 (apjeopsi juseyo) asks for a small plate to move food onto.</p>

<p>Practice here is genuinely changing. The older convention has everyone's own spoon going into the same pot, read as closeness rather than carelessness, but serving spoons and side plates are now standard in cities and among younger diners.</p>

<p>The distinction between soup words is worth knowing. Guk tends to be a lighter, more broth-forward soup served as part of a meal, tang generally refers to a heartier, longer-simmered soup, and jjigae is thicker, saltier and built for sharing. The boundaries are not rigid and usage overlaps, but the rough hierarchy helps when reading a menu.</p>


<h3>How Ordering and Paying Work</h3>

<p>Many restaurants have a call button at the table, and pressing it to summon a server is the expected behaviour rather than an imposition. Where there is no button, calling out to staff is normal and not considered rude in a casual setting.</p>

<p>Payment is frequently made at a counter near the entrance rather than at the table, and one person often pays for the group and settles up afterwards. Tipping is not customary, and adding one can create confusion rather than goodwill. Water and basic side dishes are typically included rather than charged separately.</p>

<p>Paying is a role rather than a favour, which is why itemised splitting is less common: the bill falls to the most senior person or whoever proposed the meal, and the cost rotates. Younger groups do split, and 더치페이 (deochipei) is an ordinary word.</p>


<h3>Hoesik, and the Second Round</h3>

<p>회식 (hoesik) is the company dinner, a work event held in a restaurant and often over grilled pork. Office seniority arrives at the table intact, and the grilling usually falls to the most junior person present &mdash; one of the conventions in <a href="/journal/Korean-BBQ-How-It-Actually-Works/">how Korean barbecue actually works</a>.</p>

<p>Rounds are counted. The meal is 1차 (ilcha); 2차 (icha) moves the group to a bar or a 노래방 (noraebang) karaoke room, and a third is not unusual. Each is smaller than the last, and the gap between rounds is the clean place to leave. Let the senior person start, keep nearby glasses filled, and go to the second round once, because that is where the formality drops. 먼저 가보겠습니다 (meonjeo gabogetseumnida) excuses you.</p>


<h3>Self-Serve Stations and Shoes-Off Seating</h3>

<p>Water, cups, cutlery, and sometimes extra side dishes are often self-serve from a station along the wall or a drawer built into the table. Looking for the drawer under the table edge before asking for utensils saves a wait, since it is where cutlery frequently lives.</p>

<p>Some restaurants use raised floor seating where shoes come off before stepping up. Where this is the case it is usually obvious from the shoe racks and the step, and following what other diners are doing is the simplest guide. The platform is the older 온돌 (ondol) underfloor heating arrangement, which is why socks beat bare feet.</p>


<h3>The Shape of a Korean Meal</h3>

<p>A Korean meal generally arrives close to all at once rather than in sequenced courses. The main dish, rice, soup and banchan share the table simultaneously, and you move between them by choice rather than by order. Coming from a coursed dining tradition, this can feel like everything has landed early, when in fact it has landed correctly.</p>

<p>Two further things surprise visitors. Delivery is deeply normal across an enormous range of food, and eating alone is entirely unremarkable, with many restaurants and menus built around single diners. Both reflect a food culture that is convenient and unfussy as much as it is communal.</p>
