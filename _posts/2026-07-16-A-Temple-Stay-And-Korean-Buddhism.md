<p>A 템플스테이 (templestay) is an overnight stay inside a working Korean Buddhist temple, and it is one of the few cultural programmes in Korea designed from the start with outsiders in mind. You sleep in the guest quarters, eat what the community eats, and follow a schedule that would be running whether you were there or not.</p>


<h3>What the Programme Actually Is</h3>

<p>Today the programmes are coordinated by the Cultural Corps of Korean Buddhism on behalf of the Jogye Order. The official portal groups them into one-day, experience-oriented and rest-oriented stays, with participating temples listed by region and by the languages they can support.</p>

<p>The important thing is that it is explicitly open to non-Buddhists. Nobody asks what you believe, and Korean monastics do not proselytise as a matter of practice. You are shown how to bow, sit and eat, and take part in as much or as little as you wish.</p>

<p>One assumption worth correcting: this is neither a silent retreat nor a wellness resort. These are working religious sites, not hotels. Rooms are shared and separated by gender, and bedding is a thin mattress on a heated floor.</p>


<h3>Rest-Style and Experience-Style Stays</h3>

<p>Temples offer two formats, and the difference matters more than which temple you pick. A rest-style stay, 휴식형 (hyusikhyeong), gives you meals, a room and the run of the grounds with almost nothing fixed in the timetable. You may join the dawn service, or sleep, walk and read.</p>

<p>An experience-style stay, 체험형 (chehyeomhyeong), runs a structured group programme: meditation, the formal meal, a guided walk, tea with a monk. If your interest is in understanding Korean Buddhism rather than resting somewhere beautiful, book this one. First-timers who choose rest-style sometimes come away feeling they saw the scenery and missed the substance.</p>

<p>Most programmes run one night and two days. Many temples also run a daytime version with no overnight element, a sensible way to test the water.</p>


<h3>The Shape of a Day</h3>

<p>You arrive in the afternoon, change into loose temple clothes, and get a tour of the halls. Dinner is early, the evening service follows, and lights go out at an hour that will feel absurd if you are used to city time.</p>

<p>The wake-up call comes well before sunrise, commonly around three or four in the morning depending on the temple and the season. It is announced by a monk walking the grounds striking a 목탁 (moktak), a hollow wooden hand-bell used to keep rhythm in chanting. There is no snooze. The early start is more manageable than it sounds, precisely because the evening ended so early.</p>


<h3>예불 Chanting and 참선 Sitting</h3>

<p>예불 (yebul) is the daily chanting service, held before dawn and again in the early evening. It takes place in the main hall, opens with the striking of the great bell, and consists of chanted passages punctuated by full bows. Guests stand at the back and can follow along, bow when others bow, or simply listen.</p>

<p>참선 (chamseon) is seated meditation. Korean Seon shares a lineage with Chinese Chan and Japanese Zen, and the form is familiar: cross-legged on a cushion, back upright, hands in the lap, eyes lowered rather than closed. Beginners are given a simple instruction such as counting the breath, rather than a 화두 (hwadu), the meditation question used in long-term practice.</p>

<p>Sessions for guests run ten to twenty minutes rather than the hours a resident might sit, and if your knees will not tolerate the floor you can ask for a chair. Nobody treats that as failure.</p>


<h3>발우공양: The Formal Meal</h3>

<p>발우공양 (baru gongyang) is the formal monastic meal, and for many guests it is the part they remember. You are given a nested set of four wooden bowls wrapped in cloth, and there is a fixed order for unwrapping them, receiving food, eating and clearing up, all in silence.</p>

<p>The governing rule is that nothing is wasted. You take only what you will finish. At the end you pour water into the largest bowl, clean it with a strip of radish kept back for the purpose, wipe each bowl in turn with the same water, and drink it. The water is meant to run clear. If yours is cloudy, that is visible evidence you took more than you needed.</p>

<p>The food is 사찰음식 (sachal eumsik), temple cuisine, which uses no meat or fish and also excludes the five pungent vegetables &mdash; garlic, onion, spring onion, chives and leeks &mdash; on the grounds that they agitate the mind. If you know Korean food as garlic-forward and heavily seasoned, this tastes like a separate cuisine built on mountain vegetables and fermented pastes. Restaurant manners are covered in our <a href="/journal/Korean-Dining-Customs-Explained/">Korean dining customs</a> guide.</p>

<h3>108 Bows and What They Are For</h3>

<p>The 108 bows, 백팔배 (baekpalbae), correspond to a traditional enumeration of one hundred and eight afflictions in Buddhist thought. Each bow is a full prostration: you stand with palms together, kneel, place your forehead to the mat, turn your palms upward beside your head, then rise.</p>

<p>It is demanding on the thighs and knees. Most people find that somewhere in the middle they stop counting and the movement takes over, which is the intended effect &mdash; settling the mind through repetition rather than an act of penance. Many temples have you thread a bead onto a string with each bow, so you leave with a mala you made yourself.</p>


<h3>Tea With a Monk, and Lanterns</h3>

<p>Most experience-style programmes include 다도 (dado), a tea session with a resident monk, and this is usually the part guests remember longest. It is a conversation rather than a lecture, and you can ask real questions: why someone ordained, what a monastic year looks like. Answers tend to be direct and often funny.</p>

<p>Many temples also run a lantern-making session. You build a 연등 (yeondeung), a paper lotus lantern, by gluing folded petals onto a frame, and you take it home. Lanterns are central to 연등회 (Yeondeunghoe), the festival held around Buddha's Birthday in spring, inscribed on the UNESCO list of intangible cultural heritage. Other activities vary by temple, so read the programme description.</p>


<h3>Dress, Phones and What to Pack</h3>

<p>You are lent a temple uniform, loose trousers and a vest in grey or maroon, and you wear it throughout. Underneath, cover your shoulders and knees. Shoes come off before any hall. The standard greeting is 합장 (hapjang), palms together at chest height with a slight bow, used when you meet a monk and when you enter or leave a hall. It sits alongside the conventions in our <a href="/journal/Amazing-Korean-Cultural-Tips/">Korean cultural tips</a>.</p>

<p>Two things visitors miss: the central doorway of a main hall is conventionally left for monastics, so use a side door, and you do not point your feet towards the Buddha image when seated. Phones are collected at some temples and discouraged at others, and several programmes include a period of noble silence, 묵언 (mugeon). Photography is fine in the grounds, not during services or of monks.</p>

<ul>
<li>Warm layers &mdash; mountain temples are cold outside high summer and halls are often unheated</li>
<li>Thick socks, since you will be on wooden and stone floors constantly</li>
<li>Your own toiletries and a towel; assume nothing is provided</li>
<li>Slip-on shoes, which you will remove and replace many times a day</li>
<li>A small torch for the walk to the hall before dawn</li>
<li>Any medication you need, as mountain temples are far from a pharmacy</li>
</ul>


<h3>Where to Try It First</h3>

<p>For a first attempt, a temple you can reach easily lowers the stakes. 조계사 (Jogyesa) sits in central Seoul a short walk from Insadong and runs shorter programmes. 봉은사 (Bongeunsa) is in Gangnam, opposite the COEX complex, and the contrast between the wooden halls and the office towers behind them makes the case for urban Korean Buddhism.</p>

<p>For mountains, 진관사 (Jinkwansa) sits at the edge of Bukhansan National Park inside Seoul's boundary and is known for its temple cuisine. Further out, 해인사 (Haeinsa) and 월정사 (Woljeongsa) offer the full remote experience, though read our note on <a href="/journal/Four-Seasons-In-Korea-What-To-Expect/">Korea's four seasons</a> before booking a mountain temple in deep winter.</p>

<p>Book through the official templestay portal rather than an agency. Programmes running in English are marked, and each listing states which format the temple offers. Reserve early for spring and autumn weekends, when everyone else also wants to be in the mountains.</p>
