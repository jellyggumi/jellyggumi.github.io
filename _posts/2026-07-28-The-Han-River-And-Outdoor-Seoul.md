<p>The 한강 (Hangang), the Han River, cuts Seoul into a northern and a southern half and is close to a kilometre wide in places. Visitors see it from a bridge or a train window and file it away as scenery. Residents treat it as a facility they use on a weekday evening. That difference is worth closing, because the riverside and the mountains behind it are the parts of Seoul that cost nothing.</p>


<h3>The River Is Infrastructure, Not a View</h3>

<p>Both banks are lined with a chain of eleven official Hangang parks, running from 광나루 (Gwangnaru) in the east to 강서 (Gangseo) in the west. They are public in the fullest sense: free, open through the night, and equipped with lavatories, bike repair stands, sports pitches and convenience stores.</p>

<p>The strip is far wider than the word "riverside" suggests. Between the water and the embankment there is room for football pitches, a separated cycle path, a walking path and long stretches of open lawn. The expressways sit behind and above the embankment, which is why the parks are quieter than you would expect.</p>

<p>Access is almost always by subway followed by a short walk through an underpass or over a footbridge. The parks feel cut off from the street grid, but the crossings are signposted, and getting there uses the card and network in our <a href="/journal/Getting-Around-Korea-Public-Transport-Guide/">public transport guide</a>.</p>


<h3>Which Park for What</h3>

<p>The parks are not interchangeable. Each stretch of bank has a distinct character, and picking the right one saves a long walk.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>여의도 (Yeouido)</strong> &mdash; the busiest and most central, below the financial district. Wide lawns, the most food stalls, the spring cherry blossom festival along 윤중로 (Yunjungno), and an autumn fireworks festival.</li>
<li><strong>반포 (Banpo)</strong> &mdash; the one to visit after dark, for the bridge fountain and the two lit artificial islands moored offshore.</li>
<li><strong>뚝섬 (Ttukseom)</strong> &mdash; the sports park, with windsurfing and waterskiing on that stretch of water.</li>
<li><strong>난지 (Nanji)</strong> &mdash; a campsite, below Haneul Park, a former landfill regraded into a hill of silver grass.</li>
<li><strong>선유도 (Seonyudo)</strong> &mdash; an island park built inside a decommissioned water treatment plant, the old concrete tanks left standing and planted. The quietest of them.</li>
</ul>



<h3>Cycling the Whole Length</h3>

<p>A dedicated cycle path runs along both banks with almost no interruption, separated from pedestrians. It joins Korea's national cycle route network, so the path you pick up at Yeouido continues, in principle, as far as Busan. Long-distance riders carry a certification passport and collect stamps from booths along the way.</p>

<p>Public bike hire is 따릉이 (Ttareungi), the Seoul city scheme, with docking stations at every riverside park. It works through an app with an English mode. Registering a foreign payment card is the step most likely to give trouble, so try it before you are at a dock in the rain.</p>

<p>Private rental shops near the main park entrances are the reliable fallback. They take cash, need no app, and rent what the public scheme does not: tandems, child seats and four-wheeled family pedal carts.</p>


<h3>Picnics, Delivery and Sitting on the Grass</h3>

<p>Eating on the grass is the central riverside activity, and it is organised more thoroughly than you might expect. Convenience stores in the parks sell a 돗자리 (dotjari), a foldable picnic mat, for very little. Buy one, claim a patch of lawn, and you have set up in the local manner.</p>

<p>Delivery drivers will bring food directly to you. You give the park name and a landmark &mdash; a numbered bridge pillar, a particular sign &mdash; and the order arrives on the grass. Fried chicken is the traditional choice and 짜장면 (jjajangmyeon), black bean noodles, a close second. Riverside convenience stores keep hot water machines for instant 라면 (ramyeon).</p>

<p>Drinking outdoors is broadly permitted in Korea, though the city has designated no-drinking zones, so read the signs. Rubbish goes in the bins or home with you, and open flames are confined to the barbecue areas. Wider expectations around public behaviour are in our <a href="/journal/Amazing-Korean-Cultural-Tips/">Korean cultural tips</a>.</p>

<h3>The River After Dark</h3>

<p>The parks do not empty at dusk. They fill. Paths and bridges are lit, the convenience stores stay open, and on a warm evening the lawns hold more people at ten at night than at three in the afternoon. Walking alone after dark is unremarkable here.</p>

<p>The set piece is the Moonlight Rainbow Fountain on Banpo Bridge, which sends water in an arc from the length of the span and lights it in colour. It runs on a timed schedule during the warmer months and is suspended in winter and bad weather. Watch from the riverside slope on the south bank or from the floating islands.</p>

<p>Night cycling is the other reason to come after dark, particularly in high summer, when the temperature only becomes tolerable once the sun has gone.</p>


<h3>Pools, Cruises and the Water Itself</h3>

<p>Several Hangang parks, Ttukseom and Yeouido among them, open outdoor municipal swimming pools for the summer season. They are cheap, busy and family-oriented rather than tranquil. The detail that catches visitors out is that a swimming cap is normally compulsory; they are sold on site, but knowing saves a queue.</p>

<p>Sightseeing cruises run from Yeouido and Jamsil and take about an hour, with evening departures timed for the illuminated bridges. Seoul has also run water taxi and river bus services intended as transport rather than tours, using the same transit card as the subway. Provision keeps changing, so check what is operating before planning around it.</p>

<p>The river is not for swimming. The current is stronger than the flat surface suggests, and the way onto the water is a boat or a pool.</p>


<h3>Namsan: The Mountain in the Middle</h3>

<p>Seoul was founded in a bowl ringed by mountains, and 남산 (Namsan) is the wooded hill left standing in the centre of it. It is a short climb rather than a serious one. You can walk up from Myeongdong or Jangchungdan Park, take the cable car, or ride a small yellow circular bus to the top.</p>

<p>A restored section of 한양도성 (Hanyangdoseong), the old city wall, follows the ridge. The full trail runs roughly eighteen kilometres over four inner mountains, but the Namsan section takes an hour or two in ordinary shoes. From the summit the geography resolves: the river to the south, the old city in the bowl below, mountains closing the view.</p>


<h3>Bukhansan: A Real Hike on the Subway Map</h3>

<p>북한산 (Bukhansan) is a national park inside the city boundary, which is why Seoul is sometimes called the only major capital with one. You reach the trailheads by subway plus a short bus ride, so you can leave central Seoul in the morning and be on granite before lunch.</p>

<p>The main summit, 백운대 (Baegundae), stands at 836 metres. The final section is bare rock with fixed steel cables to pull yourself up by, and it is a scramble rather than a walk. Hiking is a mainstream national pastime, so expect to be overtaken by people thirty years older than you in full technical kit. Wear proper footwear; the granite is slick when wet.</p>

<p>Gentler options exist in the park: the temple valleys are half-day walks, and the 둘레길 (dulle-gil) contours around the base without nearing a summit.</p>


<h3>Timing, and the Days to Stay Indoors</h3>

<p>Two windows are worth planning around. Cherry blossom reaches Seoul in early April and lasts roughly a week, with the riverside at Yeouido and the path around Seokchon Lake near Jamsil the heaviest concentrations. Autumn foliage peaks from late October into early November, later in the city than in the mountains further north.</p>

<p>The constraints are equally predictable. 장마 (jangma), the summer monsoon, brings sustained heavy rain from late June through July, and the lowest riverside paths close when the water rises. August is humid enough that 열대야 (yeoldaeya), tropical nights when the temperature does not fall below twenty-five degrees, are common &mdash; which is why the parks fill after dark. Fine dust is worst in late winter and spring, and on a bad day the sensible response is to postpone the mountain. Our <a href="/journal/Four-Seasons-In-Korea-What-To-Expect/">four seasons guide</a> sets out the pattern.</p>

<p>Outdoor Seoul is not a single attraction to be ticked off. It is a set of free facilities that reward a second and third visit at different hours and in different weather, and the river at nine on a summer evening is a different place from the river on an October morning.</p>
