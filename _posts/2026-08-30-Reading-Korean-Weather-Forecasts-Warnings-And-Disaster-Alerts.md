<p>It usually starts with a sound. A phone on a café table goes off at full volume with a tone that does not belong to any messaging app, every screen nearby lights up with a wall of Korean text, and half the room glances at the window to check whether the sky has an opinion. On a weekend when a stationary front is parked over the peninsula and 일기예보 (ilgi yebo, the weather forecast) is among the country's trending searches, that sound gets a lot of practice.</p>

<p>For a visitor or a new resident, the frustrating part is not the rain. It is that the whole warning apparatus — the banner on the weather app, the two-syllable words at the end of every forecast sentence, the emergency text that cannot be silenced — runs on a vocabulary of about six terms that nobody translates on the spot. The good news: behind those terms sits a fixed administrative ladder with published numbers, and once the ladder is visible, a Korean weather alert turns from noise into information.</p>

<h3>The Forecast Behind the Phone Screen</h3>

<p>Nearly every weather app in Korea, in any language, is reselling the same upstream product: the forecasts of the Korea Meteorological Administration, or 기상청 (gisangcheong), the national weather service. Its public portal is called 날씨누리 (nalssinuri), at weather.go.kr, and it carries the forecasts, the radar, and the official warning map. One practical wrinkle worth knowing: KMA states that only pages under the /w/ path of that site are currently operated, and retired pages politely answer with a notice instead of an error, so an old bookmarked link can look alive while being years out of date.</p>

<p>The words to learn first are the two rungs of the warning ladder. A 주의보 (juuibo) is an advisory, the lower rung. A 경보 (gyeongbo) is a warning, the serious one. Attach either to a hazard name — 호우 (hou, heavy rain), 폭염 (pongnyeom, heat wave), 태풍 (taepung, typhoon) — and that is the anatomy of almost every alert banner on a Korean weather screen.</p>

<h3>주의보 and 경보: The Numbers on the Ladder</h3>

<p>What makes the system readable is that the rungs are not editorial judgement. They are published numeric criteria. The Korea Meteorological Administration issues a heavy-rain advisory when 60 mm of rain is expected within three hours or 110 mm within twelve hours, and a heavy-rain warning when those figures reach 90 mm in three hours or 180 mm in twelve. The criteria are worded around expected rainfall, not measured rainfall, which is why an advisory can appear under a sky that is merely grey.</p>

<p>Typhoon criteria stack on top of the same logic: a 태풍경보 (taepung gyeongbo, typhoon warning) is issued when strong wind, high seas or storm surge are expected to reach their own warning thresholds, or when total rainfall of 200 mm or more is expected. So a typhoon warning is less a single fact about the storm and more a statement that several other warnings are about to be true at once.</p>

<p>One caveat keeps the ladder honest: it does not always have exactly two rungs. Heat has a third, higher tier, and yellow dust skips the advisory rung entirely and goes straight to warnings. The two-rung picture covers most days; it is a simplification, not the regulation.</p>

<h3>Heat Has Its Own Arithmetic</h3>

<p>Summer alerts run on a subtler number than the thermometer reading. Heat-wave reports key on 체감온도 (chegam ondo), the perceived or feels-like temperature, which folds humidity into the figure. A heat-wave advisory arrives when the daily maximum perceived temperature is expected to stay at 33°C or above for two days or more, and a warning at 35°C or above on the same two-day test. The consequence follows directly from that definition: on a muggy day an advisory can be issued even though the air temperature never touches 33°C, while on a dry day the same thermometer reading can pass without one.</p>

<p>Above the warning sits 중대경보 (jungdae gyeongbo), a serious-warning tier for extreme events. It applies only in an area that has already logged perceived temperatures of 35°C or above for two days, and then requires a perceived temperature of 38°C — or a plain air temperature of 39°C, a reminder that the old thermometer never fully left the rulebook. There is also a tropical-night report for nights that refuse to cool down, the 열대야 (yeoldaeya) phenomenon familiar to anyone who has spent an August in a Korean city.</p>

<h3>The Texts That Interrupt Everything</h3>

<p>The loud, unmutable message is a different system with a different owner. 재난문자 (jaenan munja, disaster texts) are cell-broadcast messages governed by a regulation of the Ministry of the Interior and Safety, and they cover far more than weather: evacuation orders, missing-person notices, civil-defence matters and more. The regulation sorts every alert into three classes: 위급재난문자 (wigeup jaenan munja, critical disaster texts), 긴급재난문자 (gingeup jaenan munja, emergency disaster texts) and 안전안내문자 (anjeon annae munja, safety notice texts).</p>

<p>The classes explain the volume behaviour that surprises newcomers. Critical texts cannot be refused in settings and sound at the phone's maximum volume, while emergency and safety notice texts can be switched off. A widely repeated claim online holds that critical alerts blast at 60 decibels and emergency ones at 40; the current annex of the regulation says no such thing, giving both classes the same maximum-volume rule with a 40-decibel floor. Weather crosses into this system at the top end: certain extreme-rain emergency texts are sent directly by the head of KMA, which is why a rain alert can arrive with the urgency of a public-safety broadcast.</p>

<h3>Reading It All in English</h3>

<p>KMA runs an official English service, reachable from the 날씨누리 language switcher, with current warnings under the name Current Severe Weather Status, plus short, medium and long-range forecasts, typhoon tracking and earthquake information. For day-to-day use it is a competent translation of the Korean product, and pairing it with a connectivity setup like the one in the <a href="/journal/Staying-Connected-In-Korea-SIM-Wifi-And-Apps/">SIM, wifi and apps guide</a> covers most situations a traveller will meet.</p>

<p>It is not a perfect mirror, though. The English criteria table stops at advisories and warnings, and does not carry the third heat tier or the tropical-night reports that appear on the Korean page. A reader relying on that table alone sees a slightly smaller warning system than the one actually in force, even though the live English alert feed does surface nighttime-heat advisories — a gap worth knowing about in mid-summer, when the missing tier is precisely the one being discussed.</p>

<p>For the disaster texts, the interior ministry promotes the Emergency Ready App as the foreigner-facing channel. The ministry's English site states that the app delivers emergency alerts in 22 languages, along with response guidance and shelter locations. The fine print matters: the app is an installed, permission-dependent companion, not a replacement for the cell broadcasts, so it complements the Korean-language texts rather than translating them in place on the lock screen.</p>

<h3>What Changes and What Stays</h3>

<p>The stable part of this system is the ladder itself: hazard name plus 주의보 or 경보, with published thresholds behind each rung. The changeable parts are the numbers and the plumbing. The disaster-text regulation was amended as recently as February 2026, the app's language count comes from a ministry posting dated June 2026, and the criteria table is a living administrative page — treat every figure here as correct at the time of writing and verifiable at the sources below rather than permanent.</p>

<p>Seasonality also shapes what the alerts mean in practice. The long summer rain arrives when a stationary front settles over the peninsula — forecasters now usually say 정체전선 (jeongche jeonseon, stationary front), while older KMA pages still use the classic name 장마전선 (jangma jeonseon); both refer to the same seasonal boundary. On the climatological average, the front sits over the central region from June 25 to July 26, over the southern provinces from June 23 to July 24, and over Jeju from June 19 to July 20. Rain outside that window, like a late-August soaking, comes from the same kind of front without the calendar's blessing — background covered in more detail in the <a href="/journal/Four-Seasons-In-Korea-What-To-Expect/">four seasons guide</a>.</p>

<p>None of this replaces official instructions during an actual emergency; when a text says evacuate, the reading exercise is over. But for the ordinary loud Tuesday — the advisory banner, the full-volume jolt at dinner, the neighbour calmly finishing coffee — knowing which rung of the ladder just lit up is the difference between alarm and information. The sky over the peninsula has a paperwork trail, and it is surprisingly good reading.</p>
