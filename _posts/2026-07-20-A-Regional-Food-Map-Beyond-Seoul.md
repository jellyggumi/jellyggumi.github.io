<p>Korea is a small country that eats in very different ways depending on where you stand in it. A stew in Jeolla is seasoned differently from the same stew in Gyeongsang, the noodles in Busan exist because of a specific piece of history, and Jeju cooks as an island rather than a province. Seoul has everything, which is partly a problem: the capital flattens regional cooking into a national average.</p>


<h3>Regional Cooking Is the Default, Not the Exception</h3>

<p>The Korean word for this is 향토음식 (hyangto-eumsik) &mdash; local food, food of the native soil. It describes dishes that developed in one place because of what grew there, what the sea gave up, and how far the salt had to travel inland.</p>

<p>The broad pattern is worth carrying with you. The southwestern Jeolla provinces have the most fertile farmland and richest tidal flats, and their cooking is generous, complex and heavy on fermented seafood seasoning. The southeastern Gyeongsang provinces are hotter and more mountainous, and the food is saltier and more directly spicy. Gangwon in the northeast is cold and thin-soiled, so buckwheat, potato and corn do the work rice does elsewhere. Jeju developed its own repertoire entirely.</p>

<p>So the question to ask in any Korean town is not "where is a good restaurant" but "what is this place known for". Locals answer the second readily, and the answer is a dish rather than a building.</p>


<h3>Jeonju Is a Food City First</h3>

<p>전주 (Jeonju) in North Jeolla has the strongest claim to being Korea's food capital, and it is the reason 비빔밥 (bibimbap) is famous at all. Jeonju bibimbap is a specific version rather than a generic one: rice cooked in beef broth, a wide spread of seasoned vegetables, raw or lightly cooked beef, and crucially 황포묵 (hwangpomuk), a yellow mung bean jelly that bibimbap elsewhere leaves out. It is traditionally served in a brass bowl rather than a hot stone one.</p>

<p>Jeonju was designated a UNESCO Creative City of Gastronomy, and it earns that on breadth. 콩나물국밥 (kongnamul gukbap), a bean sprout soup with rice, is the local hangover breakfast and comes in two rival styles depending on whether the egg is stirred in or served alongside. The 한옥마을 (hanok maeul) is where visitors eat, but the older market restaurants nearby are where the cooking is less adapted.</p>


<h3>Busan Eats From the Water</h3>

<p>부산 (Busan) is Korea's largest port, and its food follows from that. Raw fish, 회 (hoe), is eaten casually rather than ceremonially, and the seafood markets are working markets with restaurants attached.</p>

<p>Two dishes are properly local. 돼지국밥 (dwaeji-gukbap) is a pork broth with rice and sliced pork, seasoned at the table with shrimp paste, chives and chilli rather than entirely in the kitchen &mdash; you finish the bowl yourself, which surprises people expecting a fixed result. 밀면 (milmyeon) is Busan's wheat-flour cold noodle dish, lighter in texture than the buckwheat 냉면 it resembles.</p>


<h3>Jeju Cooks Like an Island</h3>

<p>제주 (Jeju) has volcanic soil, historically no rice paddies, and a fishing tradition led by the 해녀 (haenyeo), the women free-divers. Its food culture shares vocabulary with the mainland but little else.</p>

<p>흑돼지 (heukdwaeji), Jeju black pork, is the headline. It comes from a native breed, grilled thick and eaten with 멜젓 (meljeot), a salty anchovy sauce heated at the table, rather than the sesame oil you would get on the mainland. 갈치 (galchi), hairtail, is the island's signature fish, served grilled whole or as 갈치조림 (galchijorim), braised with radish and chilli. 한라봉 (hallabong) is a large sweet citrus named after Mount Halla, in season in winter. Look also for 고기국수 (gogi guksu), the everyday Jeju meal rather than the special one.</p>


<h3>Andong: Steamed Chicken and Inland Salt</h3>

<p>안동 (Andong) in North Gyeongsang is inland, conservative and old, and its two famous foods both come from being far from the coast. 안동찜닭 (Andong jjimdak) is chicken braised with glass noodles, potato and vegetables in a dark soy base &mdash; savoury and mildly sweet rather than spicy in its original form. It comes from the chicken alley in Andong's old market and spread nationwide from there.</p>

<p>안동간고등어 (Andong gan-godeungeo), salted mackerel, is the sharper contrast: a preserved fish dish rather than a braise. The mackerel is salt-cured, grilled and eaten with rice, and it is now one of the foods most closely associated with Andong.</p>

<h3>Gangwon: Chuncheon Chicken and Gangneung Tofu</h3>

<p>춘천 (Chuncheon) is a lake city an easy train ride east of Seoul, and it is where 닭갈비 (dakgalbi) comes from &mdash; boneless chicken stir-fried on a wide flat pan at the table with cabbage, sweet potato, rice cake and a chilli paste marinade. It is cooked in front of you like barbecue but stirred rather than turned, and the correct ending is the remaining sauce turned into fried rice on the same pan.</p>

<p>강릉 (Gangneung), on the east coast, is known for two things that seem unrelated. 초당두부 (Chodang dubu) is tofu made using seawater as the coagulant instead of mineral salts, which gives it a faintly saline taste; it is eaten plain, in soft 순두부 (sundubu) form, and increasingly in ice cream. Gangneung is also Korea's coffee town, with roasters along the Anmok beachfront predating the national café boom, which our post on <a href="/journal/Korean-Cafe-Culture-Explained/">Korean café culture</a> takes further.</p>


<h3>Mokpo and the Skate Question</h3>

<p>목포 (Mokpo) in South Jeolla, with nearby 나주 (Naju) and the island of 흑산도 (Heuksando), is the home of 홍어 (hongeo) &mdash; skate fermented until it produces ammonia. It is the most confronting food in Korea and deserves an honest description rather than an encouraging one. The smell is powerful and hits the back of the nose rather than the tongue.</p>

<p>If you want to try it fairly, do it the local way rather than the brave way. 삼합 (samhap), "three together", is a slice of fermented skate with boiled pork belly and aged kimchi eaten in one mouthful, and the pork fat and kimchi acidity blunt the ammonia considerably. Fermentation is graded, so a lightly fermented portion in Mokpo, where it is handled properly, beats a cheap plate somewhere it is not a speciality.</p>


<h3>Daegu's Heat and Gyeongju's Sweets</h3>

<p>대구 (Daegu) has a reputation for both the hottest summers on the peninsula and the spiciest food, and the two are linked in local folklore if not in agronomy. The dishes to look for are 막창 (makchang), grilled intestine, 따로국밥 (tarogukbap), a spicy beef soup served with the rice deliberately kept in a separate bowl, and the sticky braised ribs of Dongin-dong.</p>

<p>경주 (Gyeongju), the old Silla capital an hour away, is a heritage city and its food follows. 황남빵 (Hwangnam-bbang), a thin pastry packed with red bean paste and stamped on top, is the standard thing to carry home. It belongs to a category of regional confections bought as gifts rather than eaten on the spot, and every sizeable town seems to have one.</p>


<h3>How to Eat Well Outside Seoul</h3>

<p>The method is simpler than the map. Getting there is straightforward: the KTX reaches Busan, Daegu and Gyeongju quickly, Jeonju and Chuncheon have direct rail links, and intercity buses cover what the trains miss, as our <a href="/journal/Getting-Around-Korea-Public-Transport-Guide/">guide to Korean public transport</a> sets out. Once you arrive, a few habits do the work:</p>

<ul>
<li>Ask what the town is known for. The answer will be a dish, and it will be consistent.</li>
<li>Look for a street or alley named after that dish &mdash; 골목 (golmok) means alley, and food alleys are usually signposted.</li>
<li>Trust the restaurant with one page on the menu over the one with ten.</li>
<li>Eat the local speciality at lunch, when kitchens are turning over the most of it.</li>
<li>Check the traditional market first, since <a href="/journal/Street-Food-And-Traditional-Markets/">markets and street food</a> are where regional cooking survives least altered.</li>
</ul>

<p>The misconception worth correcting is that regional Korean food is a rural curiosity preserved for tourists. It is what people there eat on an ordinary Tuesday, and the reason a Busan person will talk about 밀면 with real conviction is that it is theirs. Eating along regional lines rather than by category is the difference between having Korean food and having food in Korea.</p>
