<p>A Korean bathhouse is one of the few places where a visitor's uncertainty is entirely about procedure rather than language. The rules are firm, but there are not many of them, and nobody in the building is paying you the attention you fear. What follows is the order things happen in, from the shoe locker to the checkout desk. The broader everyday conventions sit in <a href="/journal/Amazing-Korean-Cultural-Tips/">everyday Korean habits guide</a>; these are specific to the bath.</p>


<h3>A 목욕탕 and a 찜질방 Are Not the Same Building</h3>

<p>목욕탕 (mogyoktang) means bathhouse, and that is all it is: a single-sex wet floor with washing stations, a few pools at different temperatures, and lockers for your clothes. Neighbourhood ones are often small and in a basement, used the way you might use a local swimming pool &mdash; briefly, regularly, without ceremony.</p>

<p>A 찜질방 (jjimjilbang) contains a mogyoktang and adds a second layer. After bathing you put on a cotton uniform and move into a large mixed-sex common area with heated rooms, a food counter and mats to lie on. Many are open around the clock, and entry covers staying as long as you like. Places signed 사우나 (sauna) sit between the two: usually a wet area with a dry sauna cabin, not a full jjimjilbang floor. If you want twenty minutes in hot water, go to a mogyoktang; if you want an afternoon, a jjimjilbang.</p>


<h3>Shoes, Desk, Wristband</h3>

<p>Shoes come off first, at small lockers just inside the door. At the desk you say whether you want the bathhouse only or the bathhouse plus the jjimjilbang floor, since the second costs more. What you are handed depends on which:</p>

<ul>
<li>A wristband with a number on it, which is also your locker key.</li>
<li>Two towels, both smaller than you expect.</li>
<li>A cotton uniform, if you paid for the jjimjilbang floor.</li>
</ul>

<p>The wristband makes the building work. It opens the clothes locker matching its number, and at larger places it doubles as a tab: hand it over at the food counter and the charge goes against your number rather than being paid in cash. You settle up at the desk on the way out, which is why you cannot leave until you return it. Do not carry a phone or wallet into the wet area.</p>


<h3>The Wet Area Is Single-Sex, and It Is Nude</h3>

<p>The wet floor is divided by sex and everyone on it is naked &mdash; not partially covered, not wrapped in a towel. Swimwear is not a workaround. Costumes carry detergent and outdoor dirt into pools that are not chlorinated the way a swimming pool is, and the water is used by people who have just scrubbed themselves specifically in order to be clean before getting in.</p>

<p>The small towel is for washing and drying, not for covering yourself as you walk. In practice nobody looks. A bathhouse on a weekday afternoon holds grandmothers, mothers with small children, teenagers and men on their way home from work, and the register is closer to a launderette than anything else.</p>


<h3>You Wash Before You Soak</h3>

<p>Before you get into any pool, you wash, fully. This is the most important rule in the building and the one most likely to earn you a correction from a stranger. The layout is built around it: rows of low stools in front of taps, mirrors and handheld showers, with soap and shampoo provided. You sit rather than stand, which keeps the spray off your neighbours, and wash from the head down, hair included.</p>

<p>Then rinse the stool and the floor for the next person. Long hair is tied up so that it does not go in the pool, and if you have sweated in a dry sauna you rinse again before returning to the water.</p>


<h3>The Pools, and the Cold One</h3>

<p>Pools are grouped by temperature, and most places post the reading on a small sign above each, so you can choose rather than guess. There is typically a warm pool you could sit in indefinitely, a hot pool most people manage in stretches of a few minutes, and a cold pool. Anything labelled 약탕 (yaktang) is the same water with a herbal additive.</p>

<p>The cold pool is the one visitors skip and regulars do not. Alternating hot and cold is the actual point rather than an endurance test, and the loose, slightly stunned feeling afterwards is why people come twice a week for forty years. Go in slowly to the waist, breathe out, and give it fifteen seconds before deciding you hate it. Your head goes nowhere near the water in a shared pool, the small towel stays out of it, and the hot pool is closer to a quiet carriage than a jacuzzi.</p>

<h3>The Dry Floor: Uniform, Heated Rooms, Sleeping</h3>

<p>If you paid for the jjimjilbang floor, you dry off, put on the uniform and go up. It is plain cotton, colour-coded by sex, and everyone in the shared area wears it, which is why it is not optional. You will also see towels rolled into a two-lobed shape on the head, called 양머리 (yangmeori), or sheep's head. It keeps the towel out of the way and is not a joke at your expense.</p>

<p>The heated rooms are the attraction, each a small chamber with a different lining and temperature: commonly jade, salt, ochre clay, and an ice room for cooling between them. The hottest is a 맥반석 (maekbanseok) kiln room, dry enough that you take a floor mat in and low-doored enough that you duck to enter; five minutes is a reasonable first attempt. Beyond them is simply floor &mdash; heated mats, wooden head blocks, dim rooms for sleeping, massage chairs. Staying overnight is normal and inexpensive, which makes a jjimjilbang a real fallback between connections.</p>


<h3>The Food Counter Is Part of the Point</h3>

<p>The two things you will see nearly everyone holding are 식혜 (sikhye), a cold sweet rice drink with grains of rice floating in it, and 맥반석 계란 (maekbanseok gyeran), eggs slow-baked in the kiln room until the whites turn beige and the flavour goes nutty. They are eaten together, and people crave the pairing as a unit rather than as two items.</p>

<p>Past that, expect instant 라면 (ramyeon) made up at the counter, iced coffee, and at larger places a kitchen turning out 비빔밥 (bibimbap). You order, tap your wristband, and eat at a low table on the floor, which brings the usual conventions with it &mdash; <a href="/journal/Korean-Dining-Customs-Explained/">Korean dining customs</a> covers those.</p>


<h3>세신: Having Your Skin Scrubbed by a Professional</h3>

<p>세신 (sesin) is a full-body scrub performed by an attendant, and the reality is less exotic than the phrase suggests. You sign up at the desk, soak for fifteen or twenty minutes so that your skin softens, then lie on a vinyl-covered table while the attendant works over you with a coarse mitt. What comes off is dead skin, in visible grey rolls, and the quantity is startling the first time.</p>

<p>The pressure is firm and businesslike, and the attendant will move your limbs and turn you over without ceremony. It runs about half an hour. The attendants, 세신사 (sesinsa), are professionals doing a physical job at speed, so the absence of spa softness is not rudeness. If you have sensitive skin, skip it, because the scrub is abrasive by design.</p>


<h3>Tattoos, and Whether You Are Actually Welcome</h3>

<p>Tattoos are the one real access question. Older bathhouses often display a sign refusing entry to visibly tattooed customers, a rule that came from a historical association between tattoos and organised crime rather than anything about the tattoos themselves. That association has faded, but the policies survive unevenly. Small work is generally ignored and large visible pieces sometimes are not, so if it matters, ask at the desk before you pay or choose a larger modern facility.</p>

<p>The wider worry &mdash; that a foreign visitor is an unwelcome curiosity here &mdash; is misplaced. Staff deal with confused first-timers constantly and will point you at the right locker without being asked twice. What draws disapproval is procedural: skipping the wash, wearing swimwear, keeping shoes on, or being loud. Get those four right and you are simply another person having a bath. Try one in the middle of a Korean winter, for reasons <a href="/journal/Four-Seasons-In-Korea-What-To-Expect/">the four seasons post</a> makes clear.</p>
