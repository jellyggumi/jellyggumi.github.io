<p>Cafes are unusually visible in everyday Korean life. The official Service Industry Survey counted 106,452 coffee shops nationwide in 2023. That is a historical count, not the total for this year. For a visitor, the more useful question is what those rooms do beyond selling coffee. They provide a meeting place, a study desk and climate shelter. Once you understand those uses, ordering and table etiquette make much more sense.</p>


<h3>Why There Are So Many Cafes</h3>

<p>The national count establishes the scale of the industry, not the number of cafes on a particular Seoul street. For a personal perspective, Korea.net Honorary Reporter Rosemarie Ann Cuevas describes cafe-going alongside her graduate studies in the article linked below. That is the source contributor's experience, not a visit record from this site's author.</p>

<p>One reason they matter is that a cafe is neutral ground. Students, colleagues, friends and families can meet without turning a home or a restaurant table into the venue. Large low-price chains, independent shops and premium chains often sit close together because they offer different combinations of price, room, dessert and atmosphere.</p>


<h3>A Drink Usually Buys Time</h3>

<p>A cafe may offer somewhere to talk or work as well as a drink, but a purchase is not an unlimited right to a table. A posted time limit, a one-drink-per-person notice or a staff request takes priority. Before settling in with a laptop, check whether the particular room welcomes that use.</p>

<p>What people actually do in that time varies. Students spread out textbooks, freelancers work through a laptop afternoon, friends hold conversations that would be awkward in a restaurant with a queue outside, and job candidates kill the hour before an interview. Cafes also function as climate shelter: humid Korean summers and genuinely cold winters make an air-conditioned or heated room with a table a small luxury, which is worth planning around if you are visiting in the heat or the cold described in <a href="/journal/Four-Seasons-In-Korea-What-To-Expect/">the four seasons guide</a>.</p>


<h3>Study Cafes Are Not Cafes</h3>

<p>A 스터디카페 (seudeodi kape) is a study workspace rather than an ordinary coffee shop. Check its posted pricing, access method and quiet-zone rules before entering; do not assume that buying a drink buys a seat or that conversation is permitted.</p>

<p>The distinction matters when choosing a room. For a conversation, look for a cafe that welcomes groups; for concentrated work, compare the study space's seating, power access and time-based charges. A sign saying 카페 does not by itself tell you which arrangement applies.</p>


<h3>Iced Americano and 얼죽아</h3>

<p>아이스 아메리카노 (aiseu amerikano) is a familiar order in Korea, including in cold weather. The habit is well enough established to have produced its own slang: 얼죽아 (eoljuga), a compression of a phrase meaning roughly "iced americano even if I freeze to death".</p>

<p>An americano is unsweetened unless something is added, and it is a useful reference point when comparing a menu's drinks and prices. Cup sizes, extra shots and seasonal offers vary by shop, so read the board rather than assuming a standard serving or price.</p>

<h3>Check the Seasonal Menu</h3>

<p>Seasonal drinks and desserts are worth checking on the shop's current menu. The examples below are possibilities rather than a nationwide calendar: a seasonal item may sell out, stay longer or not appear at a particular branch.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Winter to early spring</strong> &mdash; strawberry cakes, tarts and lattes; 유자 (yuja) citron tea; hot chocolate</li>
  <li><strong>Summer</strong> &mdash; 빙수 (bingsu) shaved ice, peach and citrus ades, cold brew in bottles</li>
  <li><strong>Autumn</strong> &mdash; sweet potato, chestnut and pumpkin in latte and cake form</li>
  <li><strong>December</strong> &mdash; cakes sold by advance reservation, often collected on a fixed date</li>
</ul>

<p>Bingsu is worth treating as a shared dish rather than a drink. The larger sizes are built for two or more people and arrive with extra spoons, and ordering one each is usually a mistake you only make once.</p>


<h3>Dessert Cafes, Themed Rooms and Rooftops</h3>

<p>A dessert or bakery cafe may be worth choosing for the food as much as the coffee. Check the menu and display before ordering if a particular cake or pastry is the reason for the visit.</p>

<p>Alongside these, cafe-going is a recognised activity in its own right &mdash; 카페 투어 (kape tueo) &mdash; where the room is part of the point. Interiors are frequently designed to photograph well, and photography is a legitimate reason people choose one cafe over another rather than an accidental by-product. That logic produces themed cafes, cafes built around animals or characters, rooftop cafes chosen for the view, and converted 한옥 (hanok) cafes in older neighbourhoods such as Ikseon-dong and Seochon. None of this is aimed only at tourists; it is mainstream domestic behaviour.</p>


<h3>Ordering, Pagers and the Return Counter</h3>

<p>In a counter-service cafe, order and pay at the counter; other shops may use a kiosk or table service. Where seating is tight, people commonly claim a table first and then order, and leaving a bag or a jacket on a chair to hold it is understood.</p>

<p>After paying you are usually handed a 진동벨 (jindongbel), a pager that buzzes when the drink is ready, or your name or a number is called. Water, cutlery, straws, napkins and sugar are self-service from a station along the wall. When you leave, take your tray and cup to the 반납 (bannap) return counter rather than abandoning them on the table; the bins there are separated for liquid, cups and general waste. Tipping does not apply in cafes any more than it does in restaurants, and the same reasoning covered in <a href="/journal/Korean-Dining-Customs-Explained/">Korean dining customs</a> holds here.</p>


<h3>Table Time, Outlets and Wifi</h3>

<p>Table occupancy norms are generous but not unlimited. Where a cafe wants a limit it will post one, usually on the table or the door, and that notice is the rule. In the absence of a notice, read the room at peak hours: a queue of people holding trays with nowhere to sit is the signal to wrap up, and a half-empty room at three in the afternoon is not.</p>

<p>Do not assume that a seat includes a usable socket or wifi. Ask before setting up, keep cables out of walkways and use the network details provided by the cafe. A cafe's study policy can also change by room or time of day. Keep your phone, laptop and valuables with you rather than treating other customers' habits as a guarantee against theft. If you are heading out to eat afterwards, <a href="/journal/Street-Food-And-Traditional-Markets/">street food and traditional markets</a> covers another kind of informal stop.</p>
