<p>Korea has more cafes per street than almost anywhere a visitor is likely to have arrived from, and the reason is not that Koreans drink an extraordinary amount of coffee. Cafes here do a job that other kinds of space do elsewhere. Once you understand what that job is, the rest of it &mdash; how you order, how long you may sit, whether to tip &mdash; follows from it without much guesswork.</p>


<h3>Why There Are So Many Cafes</h3>

<p>In a dense Korean neighbourhood you will often pass several cafes on a single block, and one building can hold two or three of them on different floors. This is not a tourist-district effect. It holds in residential areas, near universities, beside office towers and along ordinary shopping streets, and it is one of the first things visitors notice once they stop looking for it.</p>

<p>The main driver is that homes are small and inviting acquaintances into them is far less common than in many Western cultures. Meeting someone therefore means meeting somewhere, and a cafe is the cheapest neutral ground available. It is also a low barrier business to open, so supply has comfortably kept pace with demand. Large low-price chains sit next door to independent shops and premium chains without much apparent conflict, because they are serving slightly different sittings rather than competing for the same hour.</p>


<h3>The Drink Buys the Seat</h3>

<p>The single most useful thing to understand is that in Korea a drink is effectively rent on a chair. Buying one coffee and staying for two or three hours is normal, unremarkable and broadly expected. Visitors who come from places where lingering over a single cup feels like an imposition tend to over-correct, ordering repeatedly out of guilt or leaving long before they need to.</p>

<p>What people actually do in that time varies. Students spread out textbooks, freelancers work through a laptop afternoon, friends hold conversations that would be awkward in a restaurant with a queue outside, and job candidates kill the hour before an interview. Cafes also function as climate shelter: humid Korean summers and genuinely cold winters make an air-conditioned or heated room with a table a small luxury, which is worth planning around if you are visiting in the heat or the cold described in <a href="/journal/Four-Seasons-In-Korea-What-To-Expect/">the four seasons guide</a>.</p>


<h3>Study Cafes Are Not Cafes</h3>

<p>A 스터디카페 (seudeodi kape) looks like a cafe on the sign and is not one. It is a paid workspace that happens to sell drinks, billed by time rather than by what you order, and the etiquette inside is the opposite of a normal cafe. Conversation is not welcome, seating is often individual booths with partitions and a desk lamp, and many are unstaffed, with entry through a kiosk and a card or a code.</p>

<p>The distinction matters because the two are easy to confuse from the street. If you want to talk to someone, a study cafe is the wrong room; if you want silence and a plug socket for four hours, it is much better than a normal cafe. Its older ancestor is the 독서실 (dokseosil), a spartan reading room usually taken on a monthly basis, which still exists and is aimed mostly at students preparing for examinations.</p>


<h3>Why Iced Americano Dominates the Board</h3>

<p>아이스 아메리카노 (aiseu amerikano) is the default Korean coffee order by a wide margin, and it is ordered in January as readily as in August. The habit is well enough established to have produced its own slang: 얼죽아 (eoljuga), a compression of a phrase meaning roughly "iced americano even if I freeze to death".</p>

<p>There are practical reasons behind it. It is unsweetened, so it does not fight with food. It sits at the cheap end of almost every menu. It arrives in a large cup with a lot of ice, which means it survives a long sitting without going unpleasant in the way a latte does. It also cuts through the sweet and spicy registers that dominate Korean food, which is why you will see it beside a plate of fried chicken as often as beside a laptop.</p>


<h3>The Seasonal Menu Runs on a Cycle</h3>

<p>Korean cafes rotate limited seasonal ranges aggressively, and the cycle is predictable enough to plan around. One detail catches visitors out: strawberry desserts are a cold-weather product here, not a summer one, because the domestic crop is grown under glass and peaks through winter into early spring. A strawberry cake in February is in season, and by July it has gone.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Winter to early spring</strong> &mdash; strawberry cakes, tarts and lattes; 유자 (yuja) citron tea; hot chocolate</li>
  <li><strong>Summer</strong> &mdash; 빙수 (bingsu) shaved ice, peach and citrus ades, cold brew in bottles</li>
  <li><strong>Autumn</strong> &mdash; sweet potato, chestnut and pumpkin in latte and cake form</li>
  <li><strong>December</strong> &mdash; cakes sold by advance reservation, often collected on a fixed date</li>
</ul>

<p>Bingsu is worth treating as a shared dish rather than a drink. The larger sizes are built for two or more people and arrive with extra spoons, and ordering one each is usually a mistake you only make once.</p>


<h3>Dessert Cafes, Themed Rooms and Rooftops</h3>

<p>A large share of Korean cafes are dessert-first rather than coffee-first. The cake, the croissant or the tart is the reason for the visit and the drink is the accompaniment, which is the reverse of how a European cafe usually reads. Bakery cafes with a full counter of pastry sit in the same category.</p>

<p>Alongside these, cafe-going is a recognised activity in its own right &mdash; 카페 투어 (kape tueo) &mdash; where the room is part of the point. Interiors are frequently designed to photograph well, and photography is a legitimate reason people choose one cafe over another rather than an accidental by-product. That logic produces themed cafes, cafes built around animals or characters, rooftop cafes chosen for the view, and converted 한옥 (hanok) cafes in older neighbourhoods such as Ikseon-dong and Seochon. None of this is aimed only at tourists; it is mainstream domestic behaviour.</p>


<h3>Ordering, Pagers and the Return Counter</h3>

<p>You order and pay at the counter before you sit, not at the table. Where seating is tight, people commonly claim a table first and then order, and leaving a bag or a jacket on a chair to hold it is understood.</p>

<p>After paying you are usually handed a 진동벨 (jindongbel), a pager that buzzes when the drink is ready, or your name or a number is called. Water, cutlery, straws, napkins and sugar are self-service from a station along the wall. When you leave, take your tray and cup to the 반납 (bannap) return counter rather than abandoning them on the table; the bins there are separated for liquid, cups and general waste. Tipping does not apply in cafes any more than it does in restaurants, and the same reasoning covered in <a href="/journal/Korean-Dining-Customs-Explained/">Korean dining customs</a> holds here.</p>


<h3>Table Time, Outlets and Wifi</h3>

<p>Table occupancy norms are generous but not unlimited. Where a cafe wants a limit it will post one, usually on the table or the door, and that notice is the rule. In the absence of a notice, read the room at peak hours: a queue of people holding trays with nowhere to sit is the signal to wrap up, and a half-empty room at three in the afternoon is not.</p>

<p>Power outlets are common but not guaranteed, and some cafes remove or cover them deliberately to discourage the long laptop sitting, a habit associated with the 카공족 (kagongjok), the cafe-study crowd. Korean sockets are the two-round-pin European type at 220 volts, so bring the right adapter. Wifi is close to universal and often open, with the password taped to the wall or printed on the receipt where it is not. One last observation: people here routinely leave a phone or a laptop on the table while they queue or step out, which is a genuine reflection of how safe the country feels day to day. It is still not a habit worth adopting yourself. If you are heading out to eat afterwards, <a href="/journal/Street-Food-And-Traditional-Markets/">street food and traditional markets</a> covers the other end of the spectrum.</p>
