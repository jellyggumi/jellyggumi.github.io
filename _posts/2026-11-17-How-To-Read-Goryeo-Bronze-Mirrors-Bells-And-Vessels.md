<p>In most Korean art galleries, celadon porcelain steals the spotlight with its soft jade glaze. Yet a few cases away sits a quieter family of objects from the very same Goryeo dynasty (918–1392): dark green-patinated bronze temple bells, lobed hand mirrors, tall gourd-shaped water bottles and basins traced with hair-thin silver wire.</p>

<p>Because bronze oxidizes into muted olive, brown or charcoal tones over eight centuries, visitors often pass by these pieces without realizing how tightly Goryeo metallurgy linked Buddhist ritual, court gift-giving and poetic craft. Reading three or four recurring visual details on museum labels and vessel surfaces brings an entire workshop tradition back into focus.</p>

<h3>Listening to shape: the hollow sound tube on a Goryeo temple bell</h3>

<p>When looking at a Korean Buddhist temple bell—called a 범종 (beomjong, Buddhist ritual bell)—the most reliable Korean signature sits at the very top rather than on the side. Alongside the arched dragon suspension loop, traditional Korean bells carry a vertical hollow cylinder known as an 음통 (eumtong, sound tube).</p>

<p>Cleveland Museum of Art object 1992.118 is a miniature indoor ritual bell cast in 1226 during the Goryeo period, measuring 22.6 cm tall and 14 cm in diameter. According to the museum record, it features a cast dragon loop paired with a vertical tube designed to amplify sound, while the body carries Buddhist figures seated on lotuses and an incised inscription. In Buddhist practice, the lingering resonance that fades into silence served as a physical reminder of transience and impermanence.</p>

<h3>From hanging bell to handheld ritual: the five-pronged vajra handle</h3>

<p>Not every bronze bell in a Goryeo temple hung from a wooden beam. Monks performing esoteric Buddhist rituals held smaller bronze handbells called 금강령 (geumgangnyeong, vajra-handled ritual bell), whose handles double as sacred geometry.</p>

<p>Cleveland Museum of Art object 1998.123 dates to the 1300s and measures 17.8 cm by 6.6 cm. Museum notes explain that it is called a five-pronged bell because four curved outer prongs bend inward around a central straight prong at the end of the handle, forming a vajra (thunderbolt) emblem important in esoteric Buddhism across Asia and Korea. Rather than casting a heavy acoustic chamber, the maker balanced the thin flared bell body with a weighty sculpted grip meant for rhythmic handheld ceremonies.</p>

<h3>Why museum cases display the back of bronze mirrors</h3>

<p>Visitors looking at an ancient bronze mirror often wonder why curators face the decorative pattern outward instead of the reflective surface. In pre-modern Korea, the polished front side was tin-rich bronze buffed to a mirror sheen—now darkened by age—while the cast back carried the owner's social, diplomatic and seasonal wishes.</p>

<p>Cleveland Museum of Art object 1921.660 is an eight-foiled bronze mirror from the 1100s, measuring 13.4 cm across and just 0.5 cm thick. As the museum catalog notes, bronze mirrors in Goryeo served as ritual tools, luxury goods and court gifts—historical records even describe the king bestowing inscribed bronze mirrors on the seventh day of the Lunar New Year. Look closely at the center of the cast back: a raised perforated knob held a silk cord for hanging or gripping the mirror without smudging its polished face.</p>

<h3>Shared silhouettes across bronze and celadon: the gourd-shaped ewer</h3>

<p>Goryeo metalworkers and ceramic potters did not work in isolated worlds. As tea and wine gatherings flourished among Goryeo elites, identical vessel profiles crossed directly between hammered or cast bronze and jade-glazed stoneware.</p>

<p>Cleveland Museum of Art object 1992.113 is a 33.5 cm bronze gourd-shaped bottle from the 1100s. Metal allowed workshops to stretch thin, crisp rims and towering double-bulbed proportions without slumping in a kiln—shapes that ceramicists later echoed in inlaid celadon and Buncheong stoneware bottles.</p>

<h3>Tracing silver thread in dark metal: the Eun-ipsa inlay</h3>

<p>Where celadon potters pressed white and black clay slips into carved grooves, Goryeo metalsmiths achieved delicate contrast through 은입사 (eun-ipsa, silver inlay on bronze). After chiseling shallow channels into the dark bronze wall, the artisan hammered fine silver wire into the recessed lines and burnished the surface smooth.</p>

<p>Cleveland Museum of Art object 1985.112 is a wide-mouthed bronze basin from the 1100s–1200s, measuring 17 cm high and 21.7 cm across the rim, likely used to hold purified water. Its silver inlay weaves five-character, four-line poems composed by Buddhist monks—reflecting on nonattachment and nonduality—between slender figures of children. Even after centuries of oxidation, the bright silver thread remains legible against the darkened bronze skin.</p>

<h3>Four quick cues when reading a Goryeo metalwork case</h3>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Check the top of a bell:</strong> A single vertical hollow tube beside a dragon suspension loop marks the Korean beomjong acoustic tradition, whereas a five-pronged spike marks a handheld geumgangnyeong ritual bell.</li>
  <li><strong>Look for the central boss on mirrors:</strong> The small pierced bump in the middle of an eight-foiled bronze disc is a cord anchor, proving the ornamental floral or phoenix side was meant to face outward in use.</li>
  <li><strong>Compare silhouettes across materials:</strong> Gourd-shaped bottles and lobed stands frequently bridge bronze and ceramic cases from the same century.</li>
  <li><strong>Tilt your view for silver inlay:</strong> Because silver resists corrosion better than copper-rich bronze, fine calligraphic lines on dark Goryeo ritual vessels often catch side gallery light first.</li>
</ul>
