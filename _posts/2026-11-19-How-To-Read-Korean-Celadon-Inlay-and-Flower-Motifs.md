## 1. The Jade Secret of Goryeo: Feicui Glaze and Noble Restraint

In the twelfth century, Chinese Song Dynasty connoisseur Xu Jing travelled to the Korean kingdom of Goryeo as an imperial envoy. Returning to the imperial court, he authored the *Gaoli Tujing*, recording his astonishment at Korean ceramic mastery. While Chinese kilns produced celebrated Longquan celadons, Xu Jing declared that Goryeo celadon possessed a quiet, transcendent beauty that surpassed all others, referring to its jade-green coloration as *bisaek* (비색, 翡色)—the luminous color of the kingfisher feather.

This legendary hue was achieved through meticulous reduction firing in long, sloping hillside climbing kilns (*nangama*). Potters carefully maintained iron oxide levels at approximately one to two percent in both the clay slip and the wood-ash glaze. Starved of oxygen during peak firing temperatures of roughly 1,200 degrees Celsius, iron was reduced from ferric oxide (brown/yellow) to ferrous oxide, producing a delicate, translucent blue-green glaze that appeared to hold deep water within its glassy depth.

Yet what truly established Goryeo celadon as a peerless pinnacle of world ceramic art was not the glaze alone, but the invention of *sanggam* (상감)—the technique of ceramic inlay.

## 2. The Invention of Sanggam: Carving and Two-Color Slip Inlay

Before Goryeo artisans revolutionized ceramics in the mid-twelfth century, ceramic decoration worldwide was limited to surface painting, carving in relief, or monochrome glazing. Korean potters, however, adapted indigenous lacquerware mother-of-pearl inlay techniques (*najeonchilgi*) directly into clay.

The process of *sanggam* inlay was unforgiving and required extraordinary dexterity:
1. **Incising the Leather-Hard Clay**: While the turned vessel was half-dry and leather-hard, master carvers incised hair-thin lines and floral silhouettes into the surface using bamboo needles and fine steel blades.
2. **Applying Contrasting Slips**: The carved depressions were filled with liquid white slip (purified iron-free white clay) and red slip (iron-dense ochre clay).
3. **Scraping the Surface Flat**: Once the slip set, artisans scraped away all surface excess with curved scraping knives until the original vessel surface emerged clean and sharp, with contrasting clays locked seamlessly inside the incisions.
4. **Biscuit Firing and Glazing**: After a preliminary low-temperature biscuit firing, the vessel was coated with translucent kingfisher glaze and fired in reduction. Under intense heat, the white slip turned brilliant chalk white, while the iron-rich red slip fired into deep, velvet jet black.

## 3. Iconography of the Pure Land: Flying Cranes and Chrysanthemums

When studying classical Goryeo celadon, such as the National Treasure 'Celadon Prunus Vase with Inlaid Crane and Cloud Design' (*Cheongja sanggam unhakmun maebyeong*), two recurring motifs dominate: clouds and cranes.

Cranes (*hak*) held deep resonance in Daoist and Buddhist cosmology as messengers of immortality and spiritual transcendence. Look closely at the choreography of inlaid cranes on a fine Goryeo vase: potters deliberately arranged them so that some fly upward toward the heavens while others glide downward toward earth, creating an illusion of boundless, swirling celestial space across the vessel's curved shoulder.

Complementing the cranes are stylized wild chrysanthemums (*gukhwa*) and lotus blossoms (*yeonhwa*). The chrysanthemum, blooming in late autumn frost when all other blossoms have withered, symbolized noble endurance, scholarly integrity, and quiet fortitude in the face of political adversity. Rendered in alternating medallions of white and black inlay, these blossoms created a balanced visual rhythm that anchored the dynamic movement of celestial clouds.

## 4. Spatial Harmony Between Celadon and Hanok Interiors

Goryeo celadon was never intended to sit inside brightly lit modern museum display cases. It was designed to inhabit the soft, filtered ambient illumination of traditional aristocratic residences.

Light passing through translucent mulberry paper lattice screens softened the reflective glare of the celadon glaze, causing the inlaid white cranes to glow with a faint pearlescent radiance while the black outlines receded into soft depth.

Placed upon dark lacquered wooden tables, a jade-green celadon melon-shaped ewer or delicate tea cup appeared to float like a lotus leaf upon water, creating a serene contemplative focal point that harmonized effortlessly with the surrounding timber architecture and natural silk cushions.

## 5. Practical Field Guide: Reading Goryeo Celadon Details

When admiring Goryeo celadon in museum collections worldwide, look for these subtle connoisseurship markers:

1. **Examine the Crackle (*Bingryeol*)**: Notice the fine network of glassy hairline crackles across the surface. This crazing was caused by slight differences in thermal contraction between the glaze and clay body as the kiln cooled, creating an exquisite texture resembling melting spring river ice.
2. **Inspect the Spur Marks on the Base**: Turn your attention to the underside footring. Goryeo potters supported fine celadon during firing on small fireproof white clay pads or tiny white quartz pebbles (*gyuseok*), leaving three or four neat white spur marks (*gyuseok-batchim*) that verify authentic royal reduction firing.
3. **Trace the Sanggam Boundary Lines**: Look at where white inlay meets black inlay. On genuine masterworks, the transition is sharp as a blade with zero blurring, testifying to the surgeon-like precision of Goryeo bamboo carving tools.
