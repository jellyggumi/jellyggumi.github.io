<h3>Why Seasonality Matters Here</h3>

<p>Korea has four clearly distinct seasons, and the gap between them is wide enough that the same city can feel like a different place six months apart. This is not a mild, gently shifting climate. Summer and winter sit at genuine extremes, and the two short shoulder seasons are correspondingly prized.</p>

<p>That distinctness shapes food, clothing, travel habits and even architecture. Deciding when to visit is therefore less about finding good weather and more about choosing which version of the country you want to see.</p>


<h3>Spring and the Blossom Window</h3>

<p>Spring brings mild temperatures and the cherry blossom season that draws enormous domestic crowds. The blossom window is short, often little more than a week or two in any given area, and it moves northward as the season progresses, so timing differs between the south and the capital region.</p>

<p>Two practical notes. Blossom forecasts shift year to year, so any specific date you read in advance should be treated as an estimate rather than a schedule. Spring is also the period when fine dust and air quality receive the most attention, and checking a daily air quality reading becomes a normal habit for residents.</p>


<h3>Summer, Humidity and Jangma</h3>

<p>Summer is hot and genuinely humid, and the defining feature is jangma, the East Asian monsoon rain period that typically falls across early to mid summer. Rain during this stretch is heavy rather than drizzly, and it can persist for days.</p>

<p>Air conditioning is widespread indoors, which creates a sharp contrast with the heat outside. A compact umbrella is more useful than a rain jacket, since the rain is warm and a jacket traps humidity. Late summer is also typhoon season, and while direct impacts vary considerably by year, it is worth being aware of when planning coastal travel.</p>


<h3>Autumn Foliage and Hiking</h3>

<p>Autumn is widely considered the most comfortable season, with clear skies, lower humidity and mild temperatures. It is also when the mountains turn, and foliage season is taken seriously as a travel event in its own right.</p>

<p>Korea is largely mountainous, and hiking is a mainstream national pastime rather than a niche hobby. Popular trails near major cities become genuinely busy on autumn weekends. Foliage, like blossom, moves geographically through the season, generally arriving in the north and at higher elevations first.</p>


<h3>Winter, Cold and Ondol</h3>

<p>Winter is cold and dry, and inland areas can be considerably colder than the coast. Wind matters as much as the raw temperature, and layering is more effective than a single heavy coat.</p>

<p>The great compensation is ondol, the underfloor heating tradition that shapes how Korean interiors feel. Warmth radiates from the floor rather than blowing from a vent, which is why floor seating and sleeping arrangements make sense in a Korean home. Winter is also when hot street food and hot drinks are at their best, and when the ski season runs.</p>


<h3>Packing by Season</h3>

<p>The general logic is simple. Spring and autumn call for layers and a light jacket, since mornings and evenings run much cooler than the middle of the day. Summer calls for breathable clothing and an umbrella. Winter calls for a genuine coat, plus gloves and something to cover your ears.</p>

<p>One detail that applies year-round: you will take your shoes off more often than you expect, in homes and in some restaurants and guesthouses. Footwear that comes off and on easily is quietly one of the better packing decisions.</p>


<h3>Holidays Follow the Lunar Calendar</h3>

<p>Two of the largest holidays, Seollal and Chuseok, follow the lunar calendar, which means their dates shift each year against the Gregorian calendar. Both involve significant family travel, and transport networks are at their busiest around them.</p>

<p>For a visitor this cuts both ways. Cities can feel unusually quiet while many businesses close for the holiday period, and intercity travel becomes considerably harder to book. Checking the current year's holiday dates before fixing an itinerary avoids the most common planning mistake.</p>
