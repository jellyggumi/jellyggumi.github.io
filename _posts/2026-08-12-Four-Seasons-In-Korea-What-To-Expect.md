<p>Deciding when to come to Korea matters more than it would in a milder climate. The same itinerary in Seoul is a different holiday in April and in August, and festivals, food and whether the mountains are worth the trip all hang on the season rather than the place.</p>


<h3>Why Seasonality Matters Here</h3>

<p>Korea has four clearly distinct seasons, and the gap between them is wide enough that the same city can feel like a different place six months apart. This is not a mild, gently shifting climate. Summer and winter sit at genuine extremes, and the two short shoulder seasons are correspondingly prized.</p>

<p>That distinctness shapes food, clothing, travel habits and even architecture. Deciding when to visit is therefore less about finding good weather and more about choosing which version of the country you want to see.</p>


<h3>Spring and the Blossom Window</h3>

<p>Spring brings mild temperatures and the cherry blossom season that draws enormous domestic crowds. The blossom window is short, often little more than a week or two in any given area, and it moves northward as the season progresses, so timing differs between the south and the capital region.</p>

<p>Two practical notes. Blossom forecasts shift year to year, so any specific date you read in advance should be treated as an estimate rather than a schedule. Spring is also the period when fine dust and air quality receive the most attention, and checking a daily air quality reading becomes a normal habit for residents.</p>

<p>Forecasts move because blossom responds to accumulated warmth in late winter rather than to a date, so a mild February pulls the window earlier. Bloom runs south to north: Jeju and the south coast first, the central regions next, Seoul after. Forecasters separate 개화 (gaehwa), first bloom, from 만개 (mangae), full bloom, and only days separate full bloom from falling petals.</p>


<h3>The Fine Dust Question, Honestly</h3>

<p>Two different things get folded into one complaint. 미세먼지 (misemeonji) is fine particulate pollution, measured as PM10 and PM2.5, worst from late winter through spring. 황사 (hwangsa), yellow dust, is separate: mineral sand lifted from the deserts of Mongolia and northern China on spring winds. One is smog, the other grit, and only the second turns the light yellow.</p>

<p>On severity the honest answer is that it varies enormously, day to day and year to year, and how much originates domestically rather than upwind is disputed. A bad spring week is unpleasant; plenty of spring days are clear. Residents simply check the reading, which sits in every weather app.</p>

<p>On a bad day the response is to move indoors rather than lose the day, and rated masks are sold in any pharmacy. A mountain walk keeps until the reading drops.</p>


<h3>Summer, Humidity and Jangma</h3>

<p>Summer is hot and genuinely humid. The first act is jangma, the East Asian monsoon rain period that typically runs from around late June into July. Rain in that stretch is heavy rather than drizzly, and while it was once described as days of steady rain, recent years have skewed towards shorter concentrated downpours.</p>

<p>Worth knowing: jangma is not the hottest part of summer. The most oppressive heat usually arrives <em>after</em> it, through late July and August, which is what most visitors end up experiencing as Korean summer.</p>

<p>Written 장마, the word names a condition rather than an event, and that is the distinction that matters for a trip. A rainy day passes; 장마철 (jangmacheol), the jangma season, is weeks in which the air stays saturated, laundry does not dry, and a bright humid morning still delivers a downpour by afternoon. Transport runs through it unbothered, so the real losses are mountain trails and low riverside paths.</p>

<p>Air conditioning is widespread indoors, which creates a sharp contrast with the heat outside. A compact umbrella is more useful than a rain jacket, since the rain is warm and a jacket traps humidity. Late summer is also typhoon season, and while direct impacts vary considerably by year, it is worth being aware of when planning coastal travel.</p>

<h3>Tropical Nights and Why Humidity Rules</h3>

<p>What makes Korean summer distinctive is not the peak temperature but that it does not let go overnight. A 열대야 (yeoldaeya), tropical night, is one where the temperature never drops below twenty-five degrees, and a run of them is normal in August.</p>

<p>Humidity is why this is harder to bear than the number suggests. With the air near saturation, sweat cannot evaporate, so the body loses its main cooling mechanism and the felt temperature sits above the reading. It also sets the shape of the day: people go out late and air-conditioned interiors become destinations. Our guide to <a href="/journal/The-Han-River-And-Outdoor-Seoul/">the Han River and outdoor Seoul</a> is about using the city after sunset.</p>


<h3>Autumn Foliage and Hiking</h3>

<p>Autumn is widely considered the most comfortable season, with clear skies, lower humidity and mild temperatures. It is also when the mountains turn, and foliage season is taken seriously as a travel event in its own right.</p>

<p>Korea is largely mountainous, and hiking is a mainstream national pastime rather than a niche hobby. Popular trails near major cities become genuinely busy on autumn weekends. Foliage, like blossom, moves geographically through the season, generally arriving in the north and at higher elevations first.</p>

<p>There is a mechanism behind autumn's reputation: the humid summer air mass withdraws and dry continental air replaces it, producing the run of high, clear skies. 단풍 (danpung), the turning leaves, travels opposite to the blossom &mdash; north-eastern mountains first, Seoul from late October into early November, southern peaks later. A weekday on a famous trail is a different walk from a Saturday.</p>


<h3>Winter, Cold and Ondol</h3>

<p>Winter is cold and dry, and inland areas can be considerably colder than the coast. Wind matters as much as the raw temperature, and layering is more effective than a single heavy coat.</p>

<p>Sub-zero and dry is a different cold from sub-zero and damp, and it changes what to bring rather than only how much. Dry air feels less penetrating at rest, but wind cuts through it, so a windproof shell does more than extra thickness and a thermal base layer more than a heavy jumper. Interiors are heated hard, so the layers need to come off easily.</p>

<p>The great compensation is ondol, the underfloor heating tradition that shapes how Korean interiors feel. Warmth radiates from the floor rather than blowing from a vent, which is why floor seating and sleeping arrangements make sense in a Korean home. Winter is also when hot street food and hot drinks are at their best, and when the ski season runs.</p>

<p>The 온돌 of a modern flat is a descendant rather than the original: flue gases from a kitchen firebox were once drawn beneath a stone floor, and today hot water runs through pipes in the slab. Either way the warmest surface is the one you sit on, which is why bedding goes on the floor and the spot nearest the heat was the place of honour.</p>


<h3>Packing by Season</h3>

<p>The general logic is simple. Spring and autumn call for layers and a light jacket, since mornings and evenings run much cooler than the middle of the day. Summer calls for breathable clothing and an umbrella. Winter calls for a genuine coat, plus gloves and something to cover your ears.</p>

<p>By season:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Spring</strong> &mdash; layers, a light jacket, a mask for a bad dust day.</li>
<li><strong>Summer</strong> &mdash; breathable fabrics, a compact umbrella, sleeves for aggressive air conditioning.</li>
<li><strong>Autumn</strong> &mdash; layers again, plus proper shoes if the mountains are on the list.</li>
<li><strong>Winter</strong> &mdash; a windproof coat, thermal base layer, gloves, ear cover.</li>
</ul>

<p>One detail that applies year-round: you will take your shoes off more often than you expect, in homes and in some restaurants and guesthouses. Footwear that comes off and on easily is quietly one of the better packing decisions.</p>


<h3>Holidays Follow the Lunar Calendar</h3>

<p>Two of the largest holidays, Seollal and Chuseok, follow the lunar calendar, which means their dates shift each year against the Gregorian calendar. Both involve significant family travel, and transport networks are at their busiest around them.</p>

<p>For a visitor this cuts both ways. Cities can feel unusually quiet while many businesses close for the holiday period, and intercity travel becomes considerably harder to book. Checking the current year's holiday dates before fixing an itinerary avoids the most common planning mistake.</p>
