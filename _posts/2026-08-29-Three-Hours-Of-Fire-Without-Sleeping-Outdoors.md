<p><strong>Field note:</strong> adapted from my Korean review of 20 October 2024. We paid for the visit ourselves. Every price, time and facility below is a dated observation, not a current listing; use the operator channel linked in the sources to check what exists now.</p>

<p>Autumn is my favourite season, and it is also the season that makes not owning a functioning camping habit feel like a personal failing. The weather turns, everyone starts posting photographs of tents, and I sat there each weekend wondering where we could actually go.</p>

<p>The obstacle was straightforward. INa was small. I genuinely could not remember the last time we had been camping. Setting up equipment and sleeping outdoors with a baby that age was not a plan, it was a dare.</p>

<p>What I wanted was the narrow part of camping I actually missed: charcoal, a grill, outdoor air, and a fire to stare at. Not the tent poles. Not the overnight. So we booked a day-use glamping barbecue site in Hwaseong, where all of it is set up before you arrive.</p>

<h3>The 2024 Arrangement</h3>

<p>At the time, a standard site cost 40,000 won and group space 60,000 won. Weekend and holiday visits ran in three-hour blocks. The current operator listing no longer presents that old slot structure, which is why I am recording it as history rather than advice.</p>

<p>The drive from home was about forty-five minutes before I added two pickups. Parking was modest but matched the booked capacity, so we did not circle.</p>

<h3>The Bit That Makes It Work: You Buy Everything on Arrival</h3>

<p>You check in at the main building, confirm your reservation, and buy your meat, drinks and food right there. That single arrangement is what removes the planning burden. There is no cool box to pack, no shopping trip the night before, no forgotten tongs.</p>

<p>My first reaction to the meat prices was that they seemed high. My second reaction, after eating, was that they were roughly what the same meal would cost in a restaurant. Once I framed it that way the value made sense: restaurant prices, plus a tent, a grill and an open fire.</p>

<p>The meat, prawns and mushrooms were fresh. Stew, ramyeon, rice and desserts were available too, ready to eat immediately. While we were still buying, a member of staff was outside setting up our charcoal so we could start grilling the moment we sat down.</p>

<h3>The Tent, and the Dim Light</h3>

<p>Site D had a clean tent, air conditioning and a six-person table. The inside lighting felt dim. Because we had a daytime slot and good weather, we used the table outside instead.</p>

<p>I was not drinking, as both the driver and the parent on duty. Everyone else claimed the outdoor air cancelled out the alcohol, which I record without endorsing.</p>

<h3>The Grill</h3>

<p>Staff come to your tent and explain how the grill works properly, which I appreciated more than I expected to. The system is simple once shown: meat goes on the lower level, everything else on the upper grill.</p>

<p>A self-service bar covers the supporting cast — vegetables, sauces, pickled radish wraps — and you help yourself whenever you need more. Burners, utensils, pots, blankets and water are all collected near the register. Small pot for stew, large pot for ramyeon. Water is free; you fill a bottle from the dispenser and carry it back.</p>

<p>INa fell asleep, which triggered the universal parental instruction: grill faster. My husband has been receiving a lot of praise for his grilling lately and his self-esteem is doing well.</p>

<h3>The Detail I Noticed Only as a Parent</h3>

<p>The lounge had arcade machines and the main building had a trampoline for older children. Neither mattered to our four-month-old.</p>

<p>The useful discovery was a changing table beside a small washbasin, with space to put the bag down. Before having a baby I would not have photographed it. On this visit it was the facility that made the site feel planned for families rather than merely tolerant of them.</p>

<h3>The Fire</h3>

<p>After lunch a campfire was going. We used our full three hours, but people who finished eating early had gathered around it in twos and threes, roasting sweet potatoes and talking. Marshmallows and sweet potatoes were sold for exactly that purpose, for the campfire or your own grill.</p>

<p>It was my first proper session of <em>bulmeong</em> — fire-gazing, the Korean word for sitting and staring into a flame doing nothing — since before I was pregnant. Just looking at it was restorative in a way I had underestimated.</p>

<p>We did not camp. We bought the food there, used somebody else's grill, watched the fire and drove home after three hours. With a small baby, that smaller version was the one we could actually do.</p>
