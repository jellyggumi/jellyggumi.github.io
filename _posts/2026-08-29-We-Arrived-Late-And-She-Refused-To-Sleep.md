<p>We arrived late, the baby chose feeding over sleeping and the neat newborn-photo plan disappeared almost immediately. The photographers simply worked with the baby who had shown up.</p>

<p><strong>Field note:</strong> adapted from my Korean diary of 15 July 2024, with the date checked against the care-centre and first-month entries linked below. We paid for the session ourselves. I did not record a price, and nothing here describes a current package.</p>

<p>I booked our newborn photo session before our daughter existed outside of an ultrasound image. That is the kind of parent I turned out to be. The original Korean entry describes me as a mother greedy for photographs, and I would not argue with it.</p>

<p>The booking sat in the calendar through the last weeks of pregnancy, through the birth, and through the stay at the postpartum care centre. We went straight from one to the other.</p>

<h3>Why I Booked Another Session</h3>

<p>Our postpartum care centre arranged its own newborn photographs. I still booked a separate studio while pregnant because I wanted a second set rather than one standard setup. In the original note I wrote that she looked different every day; by discharge day, two weeks already felt like several versions of the same baby.</p>

<h3>The Slot I Did Not Want to Lose</h3>

<p>The studio was close enough for discharge day and had parking. It also looked newly opened but was filling appointments quickly. I used a line in the original entry that still sounds like me: hesitation does not improve a decision; it only delays it. I booked the date we needed.</p>

<h3>We Were Late, Obviously</h3>

<p>The plan was straightforward. Leave the care centre, go home, feed her, change her, drive to the studio.</p>

<p>We did exactly that, in that order, and were still late. Traffic did the rest. The studio adjusted without making us feel hurried.</p>

<p>The lateness had one real consequence. By the time we were set up, we had arrived at the next feed. She ate. She then declined, firmly, to fall asleep — which is inconvenient, because newborn photographs largely depend on the subject being asleep.</p>

<!--post-ad-break-->

<h3>Two People, One Determined Baby</h3>

<p>The photographer and a second person handling the wraps and sets worked together to settle her. I mostly watched. It took patience, but they got the sleeping frames they needed.</p>

<p>My own commentary was less elegant. I said she looked like a little <em>tteok-dukkeobi</em>, an affectionate Korean expression for a round, sturdy-looking baby. That is the phrase the 2024 version of me chose, so it stays.</p>

<h3>The Photograph I Can Share Without Her Face</h3>

<p>The studio also took pictures on my phone while they worked, so I had a record before the finished files arrived. For this English edition I chose a close detail of her hands and feet rather than republishing her face.</p>

<h3>Listening From the Waiting Room</h3>

<p>While we waited, they were photographing older babies in the next room. I could not see it, but I could hear it clearly: the two of them working enthusiastically to make the children laugh, and the children obliging at volume.</p>

<p>I smiled in the waiting area as I listened. Two weeks into parenthood, the noise from older babies felt like a preview of a room we had not reached yet.</p>

<p>That is where the original story ends: we arrived late, she ate instead of slept, two people patiently found the frames, and I listened to the next room while they worked.</p>
