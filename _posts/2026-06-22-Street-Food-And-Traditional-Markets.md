<p>A Korean 시장 (sijang) is not a tourist attraction that happens to sell food. It is working grocery infrastructure &mdash; the place a neighbourhood buys vegetables, fish and bedding &mdash; and the food stalls exist because the people shopping and the people running the stalls need lunch. Understanding that order of events explains almost everything about how a market behaves, including why the best food is rarely on the aisle with the English signage.</p>


<h3>What a Sijang Actually Is</h3>

<p>Most established markets are arcades rather than open squares. A roof or awning runs the length of the aisles, which means a market is a reasonable destination in rain and in the worst of the summer heat, unlike an outdoor street market. Aisles are grouped by trade rather than scattered: produce sits with produce, dried goods with dried goods, then fish, then butchers, then household goods and textiles, and somewhere in the middle a 먹자골목 (meokja golmok), literally a "let's eat alley", where the cooked food concentrates.</p>

<p>That clustering is the single most useful navigational fact. If you have walked into a market and are surrounded by rubber gloves and floor mats, you have not found the wrong market; you are in the wrong aisle of the right one. Keep walking and the smell will change.</p>

<p>Outside the cities the pattern shifts. Many rural areas run a 오일장 (oiljang), a five-day market that appears in a town on dates ending in fixed digits and is otherwise an empty lot. These are worth a detour if the timing lands, because they are where genuinely local produce and regional cooking surface, the same 향토음식 (hyangto eumsik) thread covered in <a href="/journal/A-Regional-Food-Map-Beyond-Seoul/">the regional food map beyond Seoul</a>.</p>


<h3>Markets Worth Making a Trip For</h3>

<p>Markets specialise, and knowing what a market is known for saves you from ordering the wrong thing in the right place.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>광장시장 (Gwangjang Market), Seoul</strong> &mdash; the best-known food market in the country, famous for 빈대떡 (bindaetteok) mung bean pancakes, 마약김밥 (mayak gimbap) and raw beef 육회 (yukhoe)</li>
  <li><strong>남대문시장 (Namdaemun Market), Seoul</strong> &mdash; one of the oldest markets in Korea, a general goods market with a well-known braised cutlassfish alley</li>
  <li><strong>통인시장 (Tongin Market), Seoul</strong> &mdash; small, near Gyeongbokgung, known for oil-fried 기름떡볶이 (gireum tteokbokki) and a brass-token system where you buy coins at the customer centre and fill a lunch tray from participating stalls</li>
  <li><strong>망원시장 (Mangwon Market), Seoul</strong> &mdash; a residential market in Mapo, busy with locals rather than tour groups</li>
  <li><strong>자갈치시장 (Jagalchi Market), Busan</strong> &mdash; the country's best-known seafood market, where you buy fish downstairs and have it prepared upstairs</li>
  <li><strong>서문시장 (Seomun Market), Daegu</strong> &mdash; a large traditional market with a well-established night market</li>
</ul>

<p>Gwangjang is the one most visitors go to and it deserves its reputation, but it is also the most crowded and the most used to foreign customers. If you want the quieter version of the same experience, a neighbourhood market like Mangwon gives you the food without the queue.</p>


<h3>The Street Foods to Know</h3>

<p>A short list covers most of what you will actually meet. These are the canonical items, present in some form in nearly every market in the country.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>떡볶이 (tteokbokki)</strong> &mdash; chewy rice cakes in a sweet-spicy red sauce, the default Korean street food</li>
  <li><strong>어묵 (eomuk)</strong> &mdash; fish cake on a skewer, pulled from a pan of hot broth; the broth is normally free and self-served in a paper cup</li>
  <li><strong>호떡 (hotteok)</strong> &mdash; a griddled pancake filled with molten brown sugar and cinnamon, sold seed-filled in Busan; the filling is genuinely hot and worth a cautious first bite</li>
  <li><strong>붕어빵 (bungeoppang)</strong> &mdash; a fish-shaped pastry filled with red bean or custard, sold from a press by the bagful</li>
  <li><strong>김밥 (gimbap)</strong> &mdash; seaweed rice rolls, cut into rounds; the Gwangjang version is thin-rolled and dipped rather than eaten plain</li>
  <li><strong>순대 (sundae)</strong> &mdash; blood sausage with glass noodles, steamed and sliced, usually served with a dipping salt or seasoned powder</li>
  <li><strong>튀김 (twigim)</strong> &mdash; battered fried vegetables, squid and dumplings, ordered alongside tteokbokki and dipped in its sauce</li>
</ul>

<p>Two of these have a habit worth copying. Twigim is dipped straight into the tteokbokki sauce rather than eaten dry, and sundae takes salt in Seoul but is dipped in seasoned powders or sauces elsewhere depending on the region, so watch what the person next to you does.</p>

<h3>What Is Available Depends on the Month</h3>

<p>Street food is seasonal in a way that restaurant food is not, because much of it is cooked on equipment brought out for the weather. Bungeoppang and hotteok are cold-weather items, appearing as the temperature drops and vanishing in spring. So do roasted sweet potatoes and chestnuts, sold from drum roasters, and they are as much a hand-warmer as a snack.</p>

<p>Summer runs the other way, towards 빙수 (bingsu) shaved ice and cold noodles. Tteokbokki, gimbap and eomuk are year-round. If you are visiting specifically for the winter items, the timing follows the pattern set out in <a href="/journal/Four-Seasons-In-Korea-What-To-Expect/">the four seasons guide</a>.</p>


<h3>How Ordering and Paying Works</h3>

<p>Stall ordering is short and almost entirely non-verbal if it needs to be. Pointing at what you want and saying 이거 (igeo), "this one", with a number is enough. 하나 (hana) is one, 둘 (dul) is two, 세 개 (se gae) is three items. 얼마예요 (eolmayeyo) asks the price. To say you are eating at the stall rather than taking it away, 여기서 먹을게요 (yeogiseo meogeulgeyo) covers it, and the phrase set in <a href="/journal/Hangul-And-First-Korean-Phrases/">first Korean phrases</a> handles the rest.</p>

<p>Cash still matters here in a way it does not elsewhere in Korea. Card and phone payment are widespread at newer and larger stalls, but plenty of older stall holders prefer cash, and some take bank transfer or a QR code instead of card. Carry small notes rather than large ones, because a stall selling a few skewers may not want to break a big note early in the day. Most markets have an ATM somewhere near an entrance.</p>


<h3>Standing, Eating and Sharing the Space</h3>

<p>Street food in Korea is often eaten standing at the stall, not carried away. Many stalls have a narrow counter or a few stools, and the assumption is that you eat there and hand the plate or the skewer stick back. Walking through a crowded arcade holding an open cup of tteokbokki is the harder and messier option, and it is worth resisting the instinct to treat everything as takeaway.</p>

<p>The shared-space conventions catch people out more than the food does. Sauce pots, broth pans and cups at a stall are communal, and the norm is to serve yourself without returning anything to the pot &mdash; no double-dipping a part-eaten skewer, and broth ladled into your own cup rather than sipped from the pan. Bins are scarce inside markets, and used sticks and cups go back to the stall you bought them from. Seating is shared too: sitting down beside a stranger at a full counter is normal and needs no negotiation. These are extensions of the same table logic described in <a href="/journal/Korean-Dining-Customs-Explained/">Korean dining customs</a>.</p>


<h3>Telling a Good Stall From a Quiet One</h3>

<p>Turnover is the most reliable signal available to you. A stall with a queue of local shoppers is cooking constantly, which means what you get is fresh and the recipe has survived repeat customers. An empty stall with a full tray of food that has been sitting under a lamp is the opposite signal, regardless of how good the photographs on the sign look.</p>

<p>Specialisation is the second signal. A stall that sells one or two things has usually been making those things for a long time, while a stall offering the entire canonical list at once is aiming at people who do not know what they want. Watching the food being cooked in front of you is better than choosing from a picture menu, and a stall where an older proprietor is working a single griddle is generally a good bet. Aim for late morning to early afternoon, when the fryers and griddles are running for the market's own lunch crowd rather than winding down.</p>
