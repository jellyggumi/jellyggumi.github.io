<p>Korean barbecue is one of the few restaurant formats where the guest does the cooking, and that is what makes a first visit disorienting. The meat arrives raw, nobody explains the tongs, and there is a moment where you are holding a lettuce leaf without knowing what goes in it. None of it is difficult once you know the shape of the meal.</p>


<h3>The Cuts Have Names Worth Learning</h3>

<p>Ordering by cut rather than by picture is the fastest way to stop feeling lost. 삼겹살 (samgyeopsal) is pork belly, literally "three-layer meat", named for the bands of fat and lean running through it. It is the default order at most pork places and what most people mean when they say Korean barbecue.</p>

<p>Its leaner sibling is 목살 (moksal), pork neck and shoulder: a single muscle rather than layered, so it holds its shape and has more chew. 갈비 (galbi) means ribs, and the word alone is ambiguous &mdash; 돼지갈비 (dwaejigalbi) is pork, usually marinated in a soy and pear mixture, while 소갈비 (sogalbi) is beef and considerably more expensive. On beef menus, 생갈비 (saenggalbi) means unmarinated and 양념갈비 (yangnyeomgalbi) means marinated.</p>

<p>The one that catches people out is 우삼겹 (usamgyeop), thin-sliced beef brisket cut almost to paper. It looks like a lot of meat and cooks in well under a minute, so it wants attention rather than patience. Put it on, turn it once, take it off.</p>


<h3>Charcoal Versus Gas Changes the Result</h3>

<p>Look at the middle of the table before you sit down. A charcoal restaurant brings a metal bucket of glowing 참숯 (chamsut), oak charcoal, and drops it into a well beneath the grill plate. A gas restaurant has a burner plumbed in under a domed plate. Both cook meat. They do not produce the same meat.</p>

<p>Charcoal radiates heat upward at a higher intensity and dries the surface faster, which gives a firmer crust and a genuine smoke note as rendering fat drips onto the coals. Gas heats the plate itself, so the meat cooks more evenly and more gently, with less char and less smell. Neither is objectively better, but charcoal places tend to charge more.</p>

<p>The surface matters too. The plate is 불판 (bulpan), often domed or sloped with a channel around the edge so rendered fat drains away. When it blackens with burnt marinade, staff will swap it; if they have not noticed, 불판 갈아주세요 (bulpan garajuseyo) is an ordinary request.</p>


<h3>Someone Grills, and It Is Usually the Youngest</h3>

<p>Grilling is a job, and at a Korean table it tends to fall to the youngest or most junior person present. This is the same seniority logic that governs pouring drinks and starting the meal, covered in our post on <a href="/journal/Korean-Dining-Customs-Explained/">Korean dining customs</a>. It is not a punishment but a small service performed for the people above you, and doing it well is quietly noticed.</p>

<p>As a visitor you are not expected to know this, and nobody will hand you the tongs on your first night. If you are eating with Korean colleagues and are clearly the junior one, offering to grill is a good instinct. At beef specialists, staff often grill for you and cut the meat at the right moment, which is part of what you are paying for.</p>


<h3>The Scissors Are a Tool, Not a Novelty</h3>

<p>A pair of kitchen scissors arrives with the tongs, and they are for cutting meat on the grill rather than on a board. Whole strips of 삼겹살 go on long, sear on both sides, and are cut into bite-sized pieces once the outside has firmed up. Cutting too early releases juice onto the plate; too late means fighting a stiff strip.</p>

<p>Whoever is grilling also does the cutting, and the meat is cut to a size that goes into the mouth in one go. Korean table etiquette has little patience for biting a piece in half and returning the remainder to your plate, which is why the one-bite rule shows up again in the wrap.</p>


<h3>Banchan Refills Are Free</h3>

<p>The small dishes covering the table &mdash; 반찬 (banchan) &mdash; are included, and refills are free. This is a norm rather than a generosity, and it applies to the kimchi, the pickled radish, the salad, the raw garlic and the sauces. Asking for more is expected behaviour, and at busy barbecue places the same dishes are often available from a self-serve station along the wall.</p>

<p>Two refills matter especially here. 상추 (sangchu), the lettuce, and 파채 (pachae), the dressed shredded spring onion, run out faster than the meat does, and both are what stop the meal feeling heavy. 더 주세요 (deo juseyo) &mdash; more, please &mdash; with a gesture at the empty dish is enough. The one thing not free is rice: 공기밥 (gonggibap) is charged separately.</p>


<h3>Building a Wrap Properly</h3>

<p>The wrap is called 쌈 (ssam), and building one is the part most people get wrong by overfilling it. A 쌈 is one mouthful. If you cannot close your mouth around it, it is too big.</p>

<p>Lay a lettuce leaf flat across your palm. If there are 깻잎 (kkaennip), perilla leaves, put one on top &mdash; darker, serrated and far more aromatic than lettuce, and they carry fatty pork well. Then, in order:</p>

<ul>
<li>One piece of grilled meat, cut small.</li>
<li>A dab of 쌈장 on the meat, not on the leaf.</li>
<li>A slice of raw or grilled garlic, or a green chilli if you want heat.</li>
<li>A small pinch of 파채 for acidity and crunch.</li>
</ul>

<p>Fold the leaf around it, close it into a parcel, and eat the whole thing in one go. Rice is optional inside the wrap and more common at the end of the meal than the start.</p>


<h3>The Two Sauces Have Different Jobs</h3>

<p>There will usually be two dipping dishes and they are not interchangeable. 쌈장 (ssamjang) is the thick brown-red one, a mixture of 된장 (doenjang) soybean paste and 고추장 (gochujang) chilli paste with sesame and garlic worked in. It is salty and savoury, and belongs inside a wrap where the leaf balances it. As a straight dip it overwhelms the meat.</p>

<p>The other is 기름장 (gireumjang), sesame oil with salt and pepper stirred through. This one is a direct dip, and it seasons meat without hiding it. Roughly: unmarinated cuts go to 기름장, 쌈장 goes into the wrap, and marinated 갈비 needs neither.</p>


<h3>Why the Stew and Noodles Arrive Last</h3>

<p>A first-timer often assumes the soup got forgotten. It did not. At a Korean barbecue the carbohydrate and the soup are the 마무리 (mamuri), the finish, ordered towards the end of the meal on purpose.</p>

<p>The two classic finishers are 된장찌개 (doenjangjjigae), a soybean paste stew, and 냉면 (naengmyeon), cold buckwheat noodles in chilled broth. Grilled meat is rich, salty and usually eaten with soju or beer, and either a hot stew with rice or a bowl of cold sour noodles resets the palate rather than competing with it. Cold noodles after beef ribs is a specific and widely loved pairing, not an accident of the menu. Many places also offer 볶음밥 (bokkeumbap), fried rice cooked on the plate you have been grilling on.</p>


<h3>Two-Portion Minimums and the Smoke</h3>

<p>Meat is priced by portion, 인분 (inbun), and many restaurants set a two-portion minimum on any given cut &mdash; you will see 2인분 부터 or 2인 이상 on the menu. This is practical rather than a rule against solo diners: a single portion on a hot plate cooks unevenly. Solo barbecue is easier to find in Seoul, but elsewhere it is still worth eating in pairs.</p>

<p>Finally, the smoke. Every table has an extractor hood on a flexible arm, and pulling it down closer to the plate genuinely helps. It will not save your clothes. You will leave smelling of grilled pork, and restaurants know it &mdash; many provide bags for coats and aprons on request. Plan barbecue for an evening when you are heading home afterwards.</p>

<p>Once the sequence makes sense, barbecue becomes the easiest meal in Korea to eat with other people, because the cooking gives everyone something to do. Our <a href="/journal/A-Regional-Food-Map-Beyond-Seoul/">regional food map beyond Seoul</a> covers what different parts of the country are known for, and reading a menu gets easier once you can sound out cut names yourself, which is where <a href="/journal/Hangul-And-First-Korean-Phrases/">learning Hangul</a> pays off fastest.</p>
