<p>Korean is often called a hard language, and the writing system gets caught up in that reputation unfairly. The alphabet is the easy part, and it was built that way deliberately by someone who wanted it to be learnable.</p>


<h3>Hangul Is an Alphabet, Not a Character Set</h3>

<p>The most common misconception about written Korean is that it works like Chinese, where each symbol carries meaning. It does not. Hangul is a phonetic alphabet with individual letters for consonants and vowels, and it was created deliberately as a writing system rather than evolving from pictograms.</p>

<p>한글 (hangeul) was commissioned by King Sejong the Great and promulgated in the 1440s as 훈민정음 (hunminjeongeum), "the correct sounds for the instruction of the people". Korean had been written with Chinese characters, which took years of schooling, so literacy belonged to the aristocracy. Sejong wanted a script an ordinary person could learn quickly.</p>

<p>This is why learning to read Hangul is a genuinely small project compared to learning the language itself. You are learning a modest set of letters and one assembly rule, not thousands of independent symbols. Many visitors reach the point of sounding out signs within a few days.</p>


<h3>Letters Assemble Into Syllable Blocks</h3>

<p>What makes Hangul look unfamiliar is that letters are grouped into square syllable blocks rather than written in a single line. Each block is one syllable, and you read the letters inside it in a fixed order: the initial consonant, then the vowel, then any final consonant.</p>

<p>A worked example makes this concrete. In 한 (han), ㅎ is the initial consonant, ㅏ is the vowel, and ㄴ is the final consonant. In 글 (geul), ㄱ is initial, ㅡ is the vowel, and ㄹ is final. Put the two blocks together and you get 한글 &mdash; romanised <em>hangeul</em> under the official Revised Romanization, though the older spelling <em>hangul</em> is far more common in English. The blocks stack either left-to-right or top-to-bottom depending on the shape of the vowel, which is purely a layout rule.</p>


<h3>The Letters Are Shaped Like the Sounds</h3>

<p>The consonants are what make the alphabet stick, because their shapes were modelled on the speech organs that produce them. ㄱ is the tongue root blocking the throat; ㄴ the tongue tip behind the teeth; ㅁ the mouth; ㅅ a tooth; ㅇ the throat. The rest follow by adding strokes &mdash; aspirated ㅋ is ㄱ plus one &mdash; and doubling gives the tense series ㄲ and ㄸ. That is what makes the letters memorable rather than arbitrary.</p>

<p>Vowels are built from a small number of strokes: a long vertical or horizontal line with short marks added. ㅏ, ㅓ, ㅗ, ㅜ, ㅡ and ㅣ are the everyday building blocks, and the rest are formed either by adding a stroke to one of them or by combining two. Adding a stroke gives the iotised series &mdash; ㅏ becomes ㅑ, ㅓ becomes ㅕ &mdash; while combining gives compounds such as ㅘ. Once you recognise the base strokes, unfamiliar vowels become readable by decomposition rather than memorisation. The strokes have meanings: ㅡ is earth, ㅣ a standing person, and the heaven dot survives in the mark that turns ㅡ into ㅗ.</p>

<p>Consonants include ㄱ, ㄴ, ㄷ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅂ, ㅅ, ㅇ, ㅈ, ㅎ and several others. One quirk worth knowing early: ㅇ is silent when it sits in the initial position, acting as a placeholder because every syllable block needs an initial consonant slot filled. In the final position it carries an "ng" sound.</p>


<h3>Batchim, Sound Shifts and Romanisation</h3>

<p>A final consonant is called 받침 (batchim), and it is where written Korean stops matching spoken Korean neatly. Only seven sounds survive in that position, so several letters collapse together, and a final stop is not released: 밥 (bap), rice, ends closed.</p>

<p>Two shifts account for most of what a beginner mishears. A 받침 followed by a block beginning with ㅇ slides across into it, so 한국어 (hangugeo) is spoken "han-gu-geo". And a stop before a nasal turns nasal itself, which is why 감사합니다 is romanised gamsaha<em>mni</em>da rather than gamsahap-nida. 신라 reaches English as Silla for the same reason.</p>

<p>That also explains why romanisation looks inconsistent from sign to sign. Two systems are in circulation: the official Revised Romanization adopted in 2000, and the older McCune-Reischauer it replaced. Busan and Pusan, Jeju and Cheju are the same places under different rules, and names were never converted, which is why Lee, Park and Hyundai keep their old spellings.</p>


<h3>Particles, and Why Word Order Feels Loose</h3>

<p>Korean marks the job of each word with a particle attached to it rather than by position. 은/는 marks the topic, 이/가 the subject, 을/를 the object. Because the object carries a tag saying so, it can move without the sentence collapsing, which is why word order feels loose.</p>

<p>One position is fixed: the verb goes last. 저는 김치를 좋아해요 (jeoneun kimchireul joahaeyo) is literally "I kimchi like". Sentences resolve at the end, so the word that decides the meaning is the one you have not heard yet.</p>


<h3>Politeness, and the Phrases You Actually Use</h3>

<p>Korean encodes politeness in verb endings rather than only in word choice, and the same sentence can be phrased at several different levels of formality. For a visitor, this sounds more intimidating than it is in practice.</p>

<p>The practical advice is to default to the polite form ending in -요. It is appropriate with strangers, shop staff, older people and in almost any everyday situation, and it will never come across as rude. The more casual forms are for close friends and people younger than you, and the highly formal forms appear in announcements and business settings. Which form fits whom is bound up with age and rank, covered in our post on <a href="/journal/Korean-Age-Names-And-How-Addresses-Work/">Korean age, names and how addresses work</a>.</p>

<p>A short, reliable list is more useful than a long one you half-remember. 안녕하세요 (annyeonghaseyo) is the standard greeting. 감사합니다 (gamsahamnida) is thank you. 죄송합니다 (joesonghamnida) is a sincere apology, while 실례합니다 (sillyehamnida) is what you say when interrupting someone or squeezing past them.</p>

<p>For getting around, 저기요 (jeogiyo) or 여기요 (yeogiyo) is how you actually get a server's attention, and 얼마예요? (eolmayeyo) asks the price. 네 (ne) is yes and 아니요 (aniyo) is no. Romanisation here is approximate, since several Korean sounds have no exact English equivalent, and hearing them spoken is worth more than any written transcription.</p>


<h3>Two Number Systems, Two Jobs</h3>

<p>Korean uses two sets of numbers: one of native Korean origin and one derived from Sino-Korean roots. This is not redundancy, as each is used in different contexts, and using the wrong set is one of the more noticeable beginner errors.</p>

<p>Broadly, Sino-Korean numbers are used for dates, money, phone numbers and minutes, while native Korean numbers are used for counting objects, people and hours. The pattern is learned through use rather than rules, and telling time famously mixes the two: the hour takes native numbers and the minutes take Sino-Korean.</p>


<h3>Typing Hangul, and a Realistic First Week</h3>

<p>Typing is easier than it looks, because the phone assembles the blocks for you. Add a Korean keyboard in your device's language settings, then enter letters in reading order and each block forms as you go. Aspirated and tense consonants come from a long press on the plain letter.</p>

<p>A workable first week looks like this:</p>

<ul>
<li>Day one: the vowels and consonants, learned by shape rather than sound.</li>
<li>Day two: blocks with no final consonant, then blocks with one.</li>
<li>Day three: loanwords &mdash; 커피 (keopi), 버스 (beoseu) &mdash; which resolve into English and pay off immediately.</li>
<li>Day four: station names on a subway map, read out loud.</li>
<li>Day five: the aspirated and tense series, plus 받침.</li>
<li>The weekend: a menu and a shelf of <a href="/journal/Korean-Convenience-Stores-A-Practical-Guide/">convenience store</a> labels.</li>
</ul>


<h3>Why Reading Is Worth the Hour</h3>

<p>Even without speaking the language, being able to read Hangul changes a trip. Menus become navigable because many items are transliterated foreign words that suddenly resolve into something recognisable. Station names, bus destinations and convenience store labels stop being decorative shapes.</p>

<p>That is the real argument for spending an hour on the alphabet before travelling. Learning to speak Korean is a long project. Learning to read it well enough to sound out a sign is a short one, and it pays back immediately.</p>
